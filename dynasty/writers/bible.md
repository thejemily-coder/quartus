## 1. TITLE AND PITCH

**Titles considered**

- *Dominant Estate* — the legal term for the mineral estate in Texas. Accurate, cold, sounds like a law-firm newsletter.
- *The Mercy* — the ranch's name. Lovely, but it gives the irony away on the poster.
- **SURFACE RIGHTS** — chosen.

**Why:** In Texas, whoever owns what's underneath can do almost anything to whoever owns the top. That's the whole show
in two words: the land, the family, the marriage, the grave under the wedding chapel, and the woman nobody looks past.

**Series pitch**

Whitaker "Whit" Ransome has spent thirty-three years being the most charming man in any room he could leave early. Heir
to the Ransome oil-and-cattle fortune, a hundred-year-old Fort Worth dynasty with a 212,000-acre West Texas ranch called
the Mercy, Whit has never had to finish a fight, because he's never had to start one. His father, Amon, has ruled the
family like weather. Now Amon is seventy-six and newly remarried to a twenty-five-year-old professional poker player the
whole state calls the Gold Digger of the Permian. On the night Whit is supposed to be crowned, his father refuses to crown
anyone. Instead he puts the empire on trial: Whit against his sister Hadley, who is better at everything except being
loved. The prize is a company in the middle of a $3 billion deal to sell the ranch's groundwater to an AI data center,
a deal that would dry up the springs a tiny downstream village has drunk from since 1790.

*Surface Rights* is a satire of Texas money: the galas, the jets, the horse auctions, the greenwashing, the senators bought
by the pound. Underneath it is a tragedy about a man who learned very young that a nice enough story will cover anything.
His wife, Greer, would forgive him any woman except the one who makes him leave. His father is losing his mind and lying
about it. A journalist, an FBI agent and a hydrologist from the downstream village are each digging, for their own
reasons, toward the same patch of ground. And the woman Whit can't stop looking at is his father's wife. She's the most
honest person he has ever met, and she's lying to all of them. The romance is real. So is the body. Five seasons, and
every one asks the same thing: can a charming coward tell the truth before somebody else tells it for him?

## 2. SETTING

### 2.1 The family seat: the Mercy

- **Name and origin.** The family calls it "the Mercy." On old maps it's *La Merced de San Isidro*, a 1791 Spanish land
  grant (*merced* means "grant," not "mercy," and the family has never wanted to know that). Augustus Ransome got
  most of it in 1897 through a crooked resurvey and a county tax sale. The grantee families' descendants still live
  downstream.
- **Size and place.** 212,000 acres in **Cuervo County**, on the dry hinge where the Permian Basin runs into the
  Trans-Pecos. Sabinal Creek crosses it, fed by **San Isidro Springs**, three spring mouths in a limestone bowl the
  hands call *Los Ojos*. The springs have been inside the Ransome fence since 1897.
- **The Big House.** The original 1928 limestone ranch house, which Amon and Lorna wrapped in 1998 in a 31,000-sq-ft
  "Tuscan Revival" expansion: stucco, fake loggias, a wine room nobody uses, and a twelve-car garage with a
  chandelier. The family calls the old part "the House" and the new part "the Italy." Amon lives in the House.
- **The Chapel at the Mercy.** Cedar and limestone, 120 seats, built in 2003 on the site of the old stock pens, 600
  yards west of the Big House. Mercy Events LLC rents it for weddings at $38,000 a weekend ("A Hill Country Feeling,
  West Texas Soul"). Whit and Greer married there in 2019. *(HIDDEN: see §4.1.)*
- **Other places.** Mercy Field, a 5,800-ft airstrip for the G650 tail-numbered N1MRCY, which the family calls *Mercy
  One*. The Pump House at Los Ojos. The **Ransome #7 SWD**, a capped saltwater disposal well and lined pit two miles
  south of the springs. The foreman's house, where Tack has lived since 1991. Twenty-two thousand head of
  Angus-Brangus cross. A cutting-horse barn with radiant-heated floors. A helipad painted with the family brand,
  **R-Bar-M**.

### 2.2 The town: Pardee, Cuervo County

- **Pardee** (pop. 4,100) is the county seat, 95 miles southwest of **Midland**. It has a 1911 courthouse, a Dairy Queen,
  two Dollar Generals that compete with each other, and a First Baptist and a First Methodist across the street from
  each other. The Pardee Roughnecks high-school football team plays at **Ransome Field**. The 14-bed critical-access
  hospital is **Ransome Memorial**, and the library is the **Lorna W. Ransome Library**. Lorna's name stayed on it
  after the divorce, which is somehow worse.
- **New Pardee.** Austin people bought the old Ford dealership and turned it into **The Esther**, a $640-a-night
  boutique hotel with a mezcal bar and a neon sign that says *Y'ALL MEANS ALL* in a font that costs money. Man camps
  for roughnecks sit off FM 1776. The town has four economies that don't talk to each other: oil, cattle, art
  tourism and the people who clean up after all three.
- **San Isidro** (pop. ~600), four miles downstream, outside the fence. It's the old grant village: adobe and
  cinderblock, a 1790s acequia (irrigation ditch) still running off the creek, pecan orchards that went salty in 2001
  and never fully came back, and the **Capilla de San Isidro Labrador**. Many families here are descendants of the
  original grantees and of the **Sabinal Band**, a fictional Indigenous community of mission-era and Coahuiltecan
  descent. The band has petitioned for federal recognition since 1979 and holds a 1791 grant copy giving it "the
  waters of the three eyes in common forever."

### 2.3 The company: Ransome Energy, Inc.

- **History.** Founded in 1923 as Ransome & Sons Oil when the Merced No. 1 came in on the ranch, one month after Santa
  Rita. It made money through the 1930s, the 1950s boom, the 1980s bust (Amon bought distressed neighbors for pennies
  in 1986 and is still proud of it), and the shale revolution. It went public in 1997, but the family keeps 52% of the
  vote through Class B shares held by **Ransome Family Holdings LP** (RFH). Amon is RFH's general partner.
- **Today.** NYSE: RNSM. About $14B market cap. Headquarters in the **Ransome Building** in downtown Fort Worth (22
  floors; the lobby has a bronze longhorn the size of a minivan). Divisions: Permian upstream, Ransome Midstream,
  Mercy Land & Cattle, **Ransome Water Solutions** (produced-water disposal, now pivoting to "water marketing"), and
  **Ransome Horizon**, a carbon-capture initiative made up of two pilot wells, a tagline ("Energy for the Next Texas")
  and a lot of drone footage of pronghorn.
- **Current crisis (pilot).**
  1. **Succession.** Amon must name RFH's successor general partner. Everyone assumed Whit.
  2. **Project Wellspring.** A 20-year, $3.1B deal to supply **Halcyon Compute**'s 2-gigawatt "Lariat Campus" AI data
     center with gas-fired power and 9 million gallons a day of groundwater pumped from the aquifer under the Mercy.
     Texas's rule of capture makes it legal, *if* the Cuervo County Groundwater Conservation District grants the
     permit. Hydrology says it would dry up San Isidro Springs within six years. Ransome PR calls the pipeline the
     "Wellspring Community Partnership."
  3. **The short report.** *(Arrives S1E4.)* Plume Research says Ransome overstated reserves by 22% and hid four
     produced-water spills.
  4. **The predator.** Royce Teague's Teague Permian Partners is circling for a hostile bid.
- **Governance mechanics** (writers must keep these straight):
  - Amon is RFH's general partner. On his death or incapacity, the **Ransome 1987 Dynasty Trust** names the
    successor GP.
  - In August 2025 Amon amended the trust to create a **Special Trustee** with sole power to make that designation.
    The appointee's identity is sealed with Tack and a Fort Worth trust company. *(HIDDEN: §4.2.)*
  - Under her 2009 divorce decree, **Lorna** holds a 9% RFH interest with veto rights on any sale or merger. She is the
    swing vote against Teague.
  - Board: eleven directors. Six are family loyalists. Two lean Hadley: a retired ExxonMobil EVP and a Rice
    economist. Pete Ashworth (Bunny's husband, a banker) is persuadable and vain. Two are independents who'll go
    wherever the stock price goes.

### 2.4 The texture of Texas money (use liberally, specifically, and with a straight face)

- **Fort Worth vs. Dallas.** The Ransomes are Fort Worth: Westover Hills, River Crest Country Club, the Fort Worth
  Club, a table at Del Frisco's they don't have to ask for. Dallas (Lorna's Highland Park) is "where people go to be
  seen being rich." Houston is "where people go to work at being rich." Midland is "where the money comes from and
  nobody admits it."
- **The calendar.** The Mercy Ball (October, at the ranch, $25,000 a table, for "rural children's literacy"). The
  cutting-horse futurity at Will Rogers Coliseum (December). The Fort Worth Stock Show (January). Quail lease
  weekends. Dove season opening day. Galveston in August, never.
- **Objects.** Lucchese ostrich boots and custom Rios of Mercedes. A silverbelly Stetson Open Road. Topo Chico by the
  pallet. Ranch Water, which the family now sells: **Mercy Ranch Water**, canned, "made with San Isidro spring water,"
  $9.99 a four-pack, launched by cousin Colt. Gulfstreams parked at Meacham. Pecan pie from a place in Fredericksburg
  flown in by jet.
- **Money talk.** Old money says "the place" for a 200,000-acre ranch and "a little oil" for $9 billion. New money
  says "my ranch" and names the brisket. Nobody says "billion."
- **Faith and guns as branding.** Pastor Jimmy Don Quick of Lone Star Cathedral (Midland) blesses pipelines on
  camera. The Ransome Christmas card features a Bible, a rifle and a Labrador, all styled by a creative director
  from Los Angeles.
- **Legal-landscape vocabulary.** Rule of capture. Groundwater Conservation Districts. The Texas Railroad Commission
  (which regulates oil and gas, not railroads, and everyone in Texas thinks that's normal). Surface-use agreements.
  Landmen. Royalty owners. Severed mineral estates. "The surface owner has the right to be consulted, darling, not
  the right to say no."

## 3. CAST

### 3.1 Whitaker Amon "Whit" Ransome — PROTAGONIST (`whit`, not tracked)

- **Age:** 33 (born August 2, 1993). Executive VP, Corporate Affairs, Ransome Energy. That's a title invented to give
  him an office.
- **Look:** Tall, sandy, a face that photographs as trustworthy. Good suits worn a little wrong on purpose. Boots only
  when there are cameras. A scar through his left eyebrow from a polo ball at Deerfield. Smiles first, then decides
  whether he meant it.
- **Voice:** Quick, warm, self-deprecating, allergic to silence. Nicknames everyone. Says "Listen—" before a dodge and
  "Here's the thing" before a joke. Says "we" when he means "I," because "I" can be blamed. Fluent in sports, movies
  and donor-speak. When he's scared he gets *funnier*.
  - "I'm not hiding. I'm curating my presence."
  - "Dad, respectfully — and I mean that with love and genuine terror — no."
- **Want:** To be named heir without ever having to fight his father, his sister or himself for it.
- **Need:** To say one true thing out loud that costs him everything, and to survive it.
- **Wound:** Amon has always looked at him like a horse that failed to cut. Whit learned at nine that the people who
  love you will rearrange the world so you never have to look at what you did. He doesn't know that's what he
  learned.
- **Public face:** The golden boy. Board members find him delightful and assume he's unserious. They're half right.
- **Private truth:** He's smart and observant. He reads rooms better than Hadley, and he's terrified that if he ever
  tries all the way and fails, there'll be no joke left to hide behind. He's a tender, present father; June is the
  one place he never performs. He doesn't hunt, won't shoot and changes the subject when guns come up, which in his
  family is like being a vegetarian at a branding. Family legend explains it with the Coyote Story (Lorna tells it
  at parties: at nine he shot at a coyote, missed, cried, and never touched a gun again).
- **SECRETS (HIDDEN):**
  - *Known to him:* He's spent two years seeing an Austin therapist under the name "Mr. Whitaker." He once drafted a
    resignation letter and a lease for a bar in Marfa, and Greer found both (2023).
  - *Unknown to him; the series' deepest bottom layer, shared with Paloma's arc:* **On August 23, 2002, nine-year-old
    Whit shot and killed Teodoro Saldívar** with the .243 youth rifle he'd been given for his birthday three weeks
    earlier. He fired from the cab of his father's truck at dusk, believing a stranger was attacking his father.
    The Coyote Story is the lie his mother built over it, the same way the chapel was built over the body. See §4.1.

### 3.2 Greer Talmadge Ransome — HIS WIFE (`greer`, tracked)

- **Age:** 34. Born in Midland. SMU Law. Formerly a senior partner at Brightline Strategies, a Dallas crisis-PR firm,
  where she ran the narrative on a refinery fire that killed two men and got the CEO a Business Roundtable award.
  Now "Chief Strategy Advisor to the Family Office," which is unpaid and absolute.
- **Look:** Dark-blond blowout that survives wind. Tailored neutrals: camel, bone, oat. One heirloom piece of jewelry
  she bought herself and lets people assume was inherited. Perfect posture. Reading glasses she uses as a gavel.
- **Voice:** Precise. Midland vowels sanded down by law school. Speaks in agenda items. Never raises her voice; lowers
  it. Says "Mm." to mean *I heard that and I'm filing it*. Calls him "Whit" in love and "Whitaker" in business.
  - "We're not having a fight. We're having a meeting."
  - "I don't care where you put it. I care who sees you carry it."
- **Want:** The throne, through Whit: CEO, then the Senate, then whatever comes after that.
- **Need:** To be chosen for who she is rather than for what she can do. She doesn't believe anyone is capable of that.
- **Wound:** At her tenth birthday party in 1996, the repo men took the family Suburban while her friends watched
  from the trampoline. Her father, Buddy Talmadge, a wildcatter who went bust in '86 and again in 2015, still calls
  her for money. Humiliation is the only thing she truly fears.
- **Public face:** The perfect Ransome wife: a charity chair, a ferocious mother, and the one everyone assumes is
  running Whit.
- **Private truth:** She loves him. She also manages him, and for her those are the same thing. She can live with an
  affair. She can't live with an affair people see. And she won't survive him leaving, because she has rebuilt her
  whole self on the Ransome name and won't be left holding a Suburban again.
- **SECRETS (HIDDEN):**
  1. She hired a PI (Gary Huckabay, ex-Texas Ranger) to look into Paloma the week of the wedding. By S2E8 Huckabay has
     Paloma's original birth certificate (Ana Paloma Saldívar, Odessa). Greer sits on it. *(Revealed S2E8 as "she has
     something on Paloma"; contents revealed S2E10 alongside the name.)*
  2. In 2023, when she found the Marfa lease, she went to Lorna. Lorna told her: *"Then you need to know what you
     married. When he was nine he shot a man, and his father put the man in the ground."* Greer worked out in S1E1,
     when the projection went up, that the man was Teo Saldívar. **She has always known more than Whit.** Her rule
     that he never leaves is partly ambition and partly protection: she believes the family's silence is the only
     thing keeping him safe. *(Revealed S5E2.)*
  3. Party operatives have quietly courted her to run for office herself. *(Revealed S3E7.)*

### 3.3 June Ransome — THEIR DAUGHTER (`june`, tracked)

- **Age:** 5. Kindergarten at an Episcopal day school in Fort Worth.
- **Look:** Whit's hair, Greer's posture, one front tooth loose all season. Pink boots she insists on wearing with
  everything.
- **Voice:** Facts about horses and sharks. Asks "why" until she hits bedrock. Repeats adult phrases with total
  accuracy and no context.
  - "Daddy, are you lying or are you being funny?"
  - "Why does Grandpa's wife sleep in the blue room and Grandpa sleeps in the old room?"
- **Want:** Daddy at bedtime. A horse of her own (Biscuit, a retired pony). For everyone to stop whispering.
- **Need:** One parent who tells her the truth.
- **Role:** June is Whit's mirror and moral barometer. She's never a punchline, never a pawn put in danger, and
  never seriously hurt or endangered on the page. Stakes involving June are about custody, presence, what she
  overhears, and who she'll become. She adores Paloma, who treats her like a smart person.
- **Writers' note:** June's innocent observations are our best clue-delivery system (separate bedrooms, Grandpa
  "talking to the man by the fence"). Use them sparingly, about once every other episode, and never as a joke at
  her expense.

### 3.4 Amon Augustus Ransome — THE PATRIARCH (`amon`, tracked)

- **Age:** 76 (born 1950). Chairman and CEO, Ransome Energy; general partner, RFH.
- **Look:** Six-four, still. White hair cut every nine days. Big ruined hands. A silverbelly Open Road hat he's worn
  since 1974 (it has been replaced eleven times). He stands as if the floor owes him money. Now his left hand has a
  tremor he keeps in his pocket.
- **Voice:** Biblical cadence and cattle idiom, used as weapons. Long pauses that used to be strategy and are now
  sometimes the illness. Nobody can tell the difference, and that is the horror. Calls Whit "son" as a demotion.
  Calls Hadley "Hadley," in full, like a verdict.
  - "A man who needs to be liked will be owned by whoever likes him."
  - "I have buried better men than this board." *(He means it literally. Let it land as a joke the first time.)*
- **Want:** To leave the empire to someone strong enough to keep it, and to die before anyone sees him weak.
- **Need:** To confess.
- **Wound:** His own father, Augustus Jr., called him soft until the day he died. On August 23, 2002 he made his son
  carry what he did.
- **Public face:** A Texas lion, still terrifying, testing his children like yearlings before the futurity.
- **Private truth:** He's frightened, failing and in love with no one. He married Paloma because she's the only
  person alive with every reason to judge him and no reason to flatter him.
- **SECRETS (HIDDEN):**
  1. **Health.** See §4.3. Lewy body dementia diagnosed August 2025, plus severe aortic stenosis that he refuses to
     have repaired.
  2. **The pact with Paloma.** He knows exactly who she is. See §4.2.
  3. **August 23, 2002.** He started the fight, his son fired the shot, and he buried the man and the truth.
  4. **The Letter.** A sealed letter to Paloma, held by Tack, to be delivered on his death. It confesses that *he*
     killed her father. That is his final lie, told to protect Whit. *(Delivered S4E2; exposed as a lie S4E10/S5.)*
  5. The chapel was his penance. He goes there alone and sits in the back row. He has never once attended a wedding
     there.

### 3.5 Paloma Pruett Ransome — THE NEW WIFE, THE LOVE INTEREST (`paloma`, tracked)

> **WRITERS: READ THIS ENTIRE ENTRY BEFORE WRITING HER IN ANY SCENE.** Her truth is the deepest hidden canon of the
> series. It's revealed in layers, one major layer per season (§4.6). Never write her interior. Never give her a
> cutaway alone. **Rule: Paloma never lies to Whit about what she feels. She lies to him about almost everything
> else.** The romance is real from the chapel in E1. She didn't plan it, and it endangers everything she came for.

- **Age:** 25 (born March 9, 2001, Odessa, Texas). Married Amon in November 2025, eleven months before the pilot.
- **Public story:** "The card girl from Odessa." She met Amon at a private high-stakes game in the back room of the
  Petroleum Club in Midland, took $412,000 off him in one night, and he proposed nine weeks later. Fort Worth's
  favorite joke: *"He lost to her at cards and married her to get his money back."* Bunny Ashworth calls her "the
  Pigeon." The ranch hands call her *la Paloma* with something closer to respect.
- **Look:** Small, dark-haired, still. Wears her husband's money as though someone else dressed her: black, simple,
  one stray rodeo-queen detail at a time (turquoise, a man's watch). Bare face at the ranch, armor at galas. She
  turns a single worn clay poker chip (Petroleum Club, $5,000, red) over her knuckles when she's thinking. Rides
  badly and keeps trying. Swims in the stock tank at night.
- **Voice:** Low and unhurried. Asks questions instead of answering them. Treats "Okay." as a complete sentence.
  Talks in odds and tells. Speaks border Spanish with the hands and to her mare. Never small talk. Sharply funny in a
  way that makes rich people check whether they've been insulted (they have).
  - "Everyone at this party has already decided what I am. It saves me so much time."
  - "You're doing the thing where you're charming at me. It's very good. It's like watching a man tap-dance in a
    house fire."
  - (later) "I don't need you to be brave for me. I need to see you be brave once, so I know what it looks like on
    you."
- **Want (surface):** To be left alone to do whatever she's doing. **Want (true):** To find her father's body, put a
  name to what happened to him, and bring him home to San Isidro.
- **Need:** To stop gambling with her own life as the stake, and to grieve a man she never got to remember.
- **Wound:** Her father vanished when she was seventeen months old. The newspapers said he took a bribe and ran to
  Mexico. She grew up as the daughter of a thief, under her mother's maiden name, in a house where his name wasn't
  spoken. Her mother, Cheryl, drank and died in 2019 (liver), and in her last week told Paloma: *"He didn't run. They
  paid me to say he ran."*
- **Public face:** A gold digger. Cool, unbothered, overdressed for funerals and underdressed for galas, and she
  does both on purpose. *"I let them have the story. It's cheaper than correcting it."*
- **Private truth:** A brilliant mathematician (BS Mathematics, Texas Tech, finished at 20) who turned herself into a
  professional gambler because gambling was the only place where reading people paid. She came to the Ransomes as a
  player. The romance with Whit is the one hand she never meant to play. She thought he was the weakest Ransome and
  therefore the safest to use, and then he hid in the chapel with her and told her the truth about being afraid.

### 3.6 Hadley Ransome — THE RIVAL SIBLING (`hadley`, tracked)

- **Age:** 37 (born 1989). COO, Ransome Energy. Wharton MBA. Also, unlike her brother, a real hand: she can rope, cut
  cattle and pull a calf at 3 a.m.
- **Look:** Rangy, sunburned, a severe dark bob, no makeup, a Patek she never mentions. Business-casual Wranglers.
  Bites her nails to the quick.
- **Voice:** Fast, numerical. Weaponizes corporate jargon, then drops without warning into barn crudeness. Calls Whit
  "Golden."
  - "I've run the numbers on your charm, Golden. It depreciates."
  - "Dad doesn't want an heir. He wants an audience."
- **Want:** The crown. She has earned it, and everyone knows it, and it hasn't mattered once.
- **Need:** To stop needing Amon to see her and decide what she would build if no one were watching.
- **Wound:** She was the better son. At twelve she won the junior cutting championship, and Amon missed it to watch
  Whit fall off a pony.
- **Public face:** The competent, cold one. Board darling. Wall Street's preferred successor.
- **Private truth:** She's lonely, divorced (from a dentist, 2021) and furious. She loves the ranch more than the
  company, and she'd sell the oil to keep the grass. She loves her brother and resents him so much she can't tell
  the two apart.
- **SECRETS (HIDDEN):**
  1. She's been in back-channel talks with Royce Teague about a post-Amon merger in which she becomes CEO.
     *(Revealed S1E8.)*
  2. Late on August 23, 2002, at thirteen, she saw from her window the backhoe working by lantern light at the old
     stock pens. Tack told her they were burying a cow that died of blackleg. She has half believed it ever since.
     *(Revealed S3E6.)*
  3. In S3 she commissions a capacity evaluation of Amon behind his back. *(Revealed S3E8.)*

### 3.7 Odell "Tack" Tackett — THE FIXER (`tack`, tracked)

- **Age:** 67. Ranch manager of the Mercy since 1991. In practice, Amon's hands. He's dealt with thirty years of
  messes: DUIs, maids, a polo pony that died on a Saudi visit, a county commissioner, and one grave. He also has a
  law degree from Baylor (1985). He has never practiced, never mentions it, and reads every contract.
- **Look:** Small and leathery, a white mustache, reading glasses on a string. A pearl-snap shirt buttoned to the
  throat. Drives a 2004 Silverado with 600,000 miles because "a new one asks questions."

## 4. HIDDEN CANON

### 4.1 The buried crime: Teodoro Saldívar

**Who he was.** Teodoro "Teo" Saldívar, 31. Field inspector, Texas Railroad Commission, Midland district. Born in San
Isidro, the son of a grantee family, and the first in his family to go to college (Sul Ross State, geology). Married
to Cheryl Ann Pruett, 27, an Odessa waitress. Father of Ana Paloma, 17 months old.

**What he found.** From 2000 to 2002 Ransome illegally over-pressured the **Ransome #7 saltwater disposal well**, pumping
far more brine than permitted to save trucking costs during a price crash. Brine moved up an old unplugged borehole
into the shallow aquifer feeding San Isidro Springs. In 2001 San Isidro's pecans died back and the acequia turned
brackish. Teo documented it and filed a preliminary report, which disappeared from the district office. On August
23, 2002 he drove back out at dusk to photograph the over-full overflow pit with his own camera.

**What really happened (August 23, 2002, ~8:20 p.m.):**

1. Amon, tipped off by Tack that a state truck had gone through the south gate, drove out to the #7 pit in his ranch
   truck. Nine-year-old Whit was in the cab; Amon had taken him along "to check water," with the .243 youth rifle
   from Whit's ninth birthday on the rack and a promise of jackrabbits.
2. Amon ordered Teo off the land. Teo refused, calmly, and said he was there under state authority. Amon grabbed for
   the camera, Teo pulled it back, and Amon swung at him. Teo shoved him away. Amon, 52, fell hard on the caliche
   and cried out.
3. In the dusk, the boy saw a stranger standing over his fallen father. He got the rifle off the rack, rested it on
   the open window and fired once. The bullet hit Teo in the chest. He died within a minute, with Amon kneeling over
   him.
4. Amon carried his son into the House. Lorna took over. Tack came with the ranch backhoe. That night, under
   lanterns at the old stock pens, Tack and Amon buried Teo. Hadley, 13, saw the lights from her window.
5. Lorna sat up all night with Whit and told him, again and again, *"You shot at a coyote, baby. You missed. Daddy
   was mad because you could have hurt the horses."* She told it until it became the only version he had. The rifle
   "got lost." Whit never touched a gun again.
6. Tack drove Teo's 1994 Silverado to Ojinaga and abandoned it. Amon's attorney, Judge Everett Pyle (died 2017),
   arranged a $48,000 cash deposit into a Banco Azteca account opened in Teo's name with a forged ID. Deputy Boyd
   Cantrell wrote up "voluntary departure." Pyle fed Mags the "bribery and flight" tip. The RRC took a quiet
   resignation letter "from" Teo and buried the #7 file. A 2003 inspection by a different inspector found the well
   "in compliance." That inspector joined Ransome Water in 2005.
7. In February 2003 the old stock pens were bulldozed and **the Chapel at the Mercy** was poured on top of them. Lorna
   had the slab extended eighteen feet west, over the grave. **Teo lies under the chapel's west end, beneath the
   spot where wedding couples stand.**

### 4.3 The patriarch's health

  he sees **a man standing at the fence line** or in the back of the chapel. That man is Teo, but no one but Amon

### 4.4 Land, water and mineral rights

- **The fight:** Project Wellspring wants 9 MGD from the aquifer under the Mercy. The Cuervo County Groundwater
  Conservation District board has five members; the Ransomes control three through appointments Kilgore fixed with
  bribes. Ojo Vivo and the Sabinal Band argue that (1) the 1791 grant reserved "the waters of the three eyes in
  common forever," (2) the Ransome title stems from a fraudulent 1897 resurvey, and (3) the 2000–2002 brine
  contamination (if proven) voids the company's water-marketing permits and creates liability.
- **The moral stakes, plainly:** If Wellspring goes ahead, a 230-year-old village loses its springs so a chatbot farm
  can run. If it dies, Ransome loses $3.1B, Halcyon builds in New Mexico, and 400 promised jobs in Pardee, which the
  town desperately wants, vanish with it. San Isidro isn't unanimous either: some families want the buyout money.
  Delfina's movement is partly funded by a rival hyperscaler. No one is clean, but the water is real, and so is
  whose it was.
- **The convergence:** The Wellspring pipeline easement surveyed in S1E7 runs straight across the chapel lot. Amon
  refuses to move a single stake, which baffles the board, and that is a clue. In S4, ground-penetrating radar for
  the re-routed easement (or Paloma's own dig) finds the body. Greenwashing literally unearths the crime.
- **Endgame lever:** Whoever controls RFH can deed San Isidro Springs and a riparian corridor to the Sabinal Band (the
  "San Isidro Conveyance"). Paloma has a drafted conveyance from S3E7. Whit, Hadley or Greer can sign it, sit on it
  or burn it.

## 6. FIVE-SEASON MAP

**Season One — "The Trial."** *Central question:* Who gets the crown? At the Mercy Ball, Amon refuses to name Whit his
heir and sets a year-end test. Hadley gets Wellspring (the money) and Whit gets San Isidro (the mess). Whit meets his
father's wife properly in the one place they're both hiding, and nothing physical happens for ten episodes except
everything. Mags arrives; a short-seller bleeds the stock; Kilgore is groomed for the Senate. *Midpoint (E5–E6):* the
prenup turns out to give Paloma $1 and a mare, so the gold digger wants something money can't buy, and in Houston
Whit learns his father's mind is going. He becomes Paloma's co-conspirator in hiding it. *Finale (E10):* At the
annual meeting Amon has a public episode. Whit either covers for him or lets it show, and Amon reveals that a sealed
Special Trustee will choose his heir. That night, in the chapel, Whit and Paloma kiss for the first time if the player
has earned it; otherwise he takes her hand and doesn't let go. Mags opens an anonymous envelope with Teo Saldívar's
last inspection schedule. Greer, awake when Whit comes home, says only: *"Whatever it is, don't ever leave."*

**Season Two — "The Primary."** *Central question:* What will you risk for what you want? The affair becomes real. It's
reckless and tender, and it's a secret kept inside a house full of cameras, nannies, influencers and a wired cousin.
The Senate primary turns the family into a campaign. Mags publishes Part One of "The Inspector" with Teo's voicemail.
Garza reveals his 2002 report. Delfina files the grant-water suit and learns who's really funding her. *Midpoint
(E5–E6):* consummation, at the earliest (charged then cut), at the old line shack during a norther. In the same
episode Amon makes Whit acting CEO for thirty days as a test, and Whit is good at it. That scares him most of all.
*Finale (E10):* Greer lays out her terms ("Have her. Never leave."). Then, in Delfina's grandmother's house, Whit sees
a photograph of Teo Saldívar holding his baby daughter and understands who Paloma is. The season ends on the
question the player has to carry into S3: *Did she come here for him, or through him?*

**Season Three — "The Trustee."** *Central question:* What does a king owe? Amon declines, sharp and then gone. The
truth comes out (E4): the marriage is a pact, unconsummated, made with a man who knew exactly whose daughter he was
marrying, and Paloma holds the crown in her hands. Whit must decide whether her love is real (it is) and whether
he can bear being judged by her. Hadley sues for capacity. Kilgore is indicted (E5), and Greer moves to make Whit
the replacement nominee, which forces the coward onto every screen in Texas. *Midpoint (E5–E6):* the indictment, and
Hadley's drunken confession that she saw a backhoe the summer she was thirteen. Teague is wired. Amon's last lucid
conversation with Whit (if earned) is about a quail lease in 1999, and about nothing else, and about everything.
*Finale (E10):* Amon dies at 3:58 a.m. with Paloma beside him. She calls at 4:38. Whit arrives at dawn to find her
sitting on the steps of the House in her nightgown and his father's hat.

**Season Four — "Exhumation."** *Central question:* What's buried? The funeral is a livestreamed gala with a gospel
choir and a riderless horse whose handler has an NDA. Paloma, as Special Trustee, faces the family. The Letter tells
her where her father is and claims Amon killed him. She was Mags's original source (E3); the slab was extended "per
Mrs. R." (E4). *Midpoint (E5):* radar and shovels at the chapel's west end; the body is found under the spot where
Whit and Greer took their vows. Pardee becomes a cable-news set. San Isidro holds a vigil. Paloma learns her
childhood was bought (E6). Election Day (E8) comes the same week the forty minutes are exposed and Hadley accuses
Paloma of killing Amon. Paloma develops her father's last roll (E9) and says nothing about the last frame.
*Finale (E10):* ballistics come back .243. In the cab of his 600,000-mile Silverado, Tack hands Whit a long oilcloth
bundle: *"It was yours, Bud. Your mama told you it was a coyote."* The season ends on Whit's hands, and on the
coyote, finally seen.

**Season Five — "The Coyote."** *Central question:* Can a coward tell the truth before someone tells it for him? Whit
knows. Lorna admits the lie (E1). Greer has known since 2023 (E2) and offers to keep the family's silence forever as
long as he stays. Garza tells him (E4) that a child under ten can't be charged; the only thing left to lose is
everything else. Teague makes a final bid. The water verdict arrives (E7). *Midpoint (E5–E6):* the window in which
Whit can tell Paloma himself, before Greer or Lorna weaponizes it. *Turn (E8):* Paloma has known since the film,
and she has been waiting. *Finale (E9–E10):* confession, conveyance and crown, in the player's order. **The romantic
core can reach a satisfying end:** if Whit confesses to Paloma himself and then publicly, signs the San Isidro
Conveyance and gives up the crown (Greer takes it, and she's magnificent), the last scene is Whit, Paloma and Teo's
reburial at the Capilla de San Isidro. June runs ahead in her pink boots toward the three eyes of the springs, which
are running. Other endings: **The Crown** (he keeps the secret, keeps the throne and Greer; Paloma leaves with the
truth and says so in a single letter); **Surface Rights** (Teague owns everything and everyone loses); **The
Witness** (he confesses too late; she forgives him and doesn't stay).

## 8. STYLE GUIDE

### 8.1 Prose

- **Close third on Whit, present tense.** Present tense because this is interactive: the player is choosing *now*, and
  past tense implies the outcome already happened. Present also sharpens dread. When a cutaway jumps to Tack in the
  chapel at 3 a.m., it reads as happening *while* Whit sleeps.
- We know only what Whit knows, notices or misreads. Whit's misreadings are a tool: he will be wrong about
  Paloma's reactions in exactly the ways she's counting on.
- **Satire at the sentence level, not the premise level.** The joke is in the precise detail: the chandelier in the
  garage, the dead quail from the freezer, the font on *Y'ALL MEANS ALL*. State absurdities flatly and never wink.
  Let the rich say awful things sincerely.
- **Comedy curdles.** A scene can open funny and end cold, and the best ones do. The turn happens in a single line.
- **Dialogue lives inside prose paragraphs**, novel-style, with typographic quotes. Short paragraphs; three to five
  sentences is the rhythm. Vary sentence length on purpose, and use a one-line paragraph for a blow.
- **Scene slugs** in screenplay style: `INT. THE CHAPEL AT THE MERCY — NIGHT`, `EXT. SAN ISIDRO ACEQUIA — DAWN`,
  `INT. MERCY ONE — 41,000 FEET — DAY`.
- **Cutaways:** at most 1–2 per episode. Always `cutaway: true`. Always dread: a gesture, an object, a place. Never
  hidden-canon facts stated outright. **Never Paloma alone. Never her interior.** Prefer Tack, Lorna, Garza,
  Huckabay, Amon, Greer.
- **Specificity rule:** Every scene needs one concrete, real-feeling Texas detail (a brand, a road number, a dish, a
  weather fact) and one concrete detail of money.

### 8.2 Choices in Whit's idiom

- Choices are short, active and in his voice: *"Make it a joke."* · *"Tell her the truth. Most of it."* · *"Pour two
  fingers and stall."* · *"Say 'Dad.' Just that."* · *"Smile. Leave."* · *"Ask her what she wants. Wait for the
  answer."*
- Most charged moments offer three textures: **the dodge** (charming, tempting, often rewarded locally), **the true
  thing** (costs now, pays later), and **the move** (strategic or cruel). Don't label them. The player should feel
  the pull of the dodge, because that's his flaw.
- The dodge must never be the obviously "wrong" option. It's often the funniest line on the screen.
- Converge freely with `goto` the same scene and let tone and flags carry the difference. Branch scenes only for
  real forks (the kiss, cover vs. expose, telling Greer).

### 8.3 Cues

- Telltale-style, short, character-first, present or future: *"Paloma will remember that."* · *"Greer noticed."* ·
  *"Your father saw you hesitate."* · *"Hadley won't forget this."* · *"June heard every word."* · *"Tack said
  nothing. He usually doesn't."*
- No numbers, no "+1," no "trust increased." Cue a big moment, not every choice; at most one cue per scene.
- Cues must never leak hidden canon. ("Paloma will remember that" is fine. "Paloma's father would have liked that"
  is not.)

### 8.4 Intimacy under the content limits

- All romantic and sexual characters are adults; Paloma is 25 and Whit is 33. No sexual content involves anyone
  under 18, ever.
- **Charged, then cut.** The lead-in can be frank: desire, nerves, what's said, hands, breath, the decision. Cut
  before the act. The aftermath can be frank too: what's raw, funny or frightening, what they finally say, the
  intimacy of afterward. **Never graphic or explicit description of sex.**
- Intimacy is about character. Every intimate beat should change something between them: a truth told, a lie
  noticed, a line crossed.
- Small moments are the romance's real currency: a borrowed jacket, the chip passed across knuckles, a word of
  Spanish he finally gets right, her laugh at something only he said, a hand at the small of a back in a crowded
  room.
- **Consummation not before S2** (earliest S2E5). S1 is restraint, so the kiss in S1E10 is the most a player can
  earn.
- Paloma consents freely and clearly; so does Whit. Nothing is coerced, transactional or traded for information.

### 8.5 Satire: do and don't

- **Do** punch up: at the rich, the bought, the costumed, the greenwashers, Kilgore's horse handler, Soren's
  cathedrals of inference, the livestreamed funeral.
- **Do** let the satire's targets be human. Amon is grand and frightened, Greer is right about almost everything,
  and Colt is sweet.
- **Do** keep the water fight morally real: jobs vs. springs, a village that isn't unanimous, an activist funded by
  the wrong money.
- **Don't** mock San Isidro, its poverty, its faith or its people. The Sabinal Band isn't mystical, isn't a prop and
  isn't a chorus of noble victims. Its members are lawyers, hydrologists, aunts and a teenager who wants the buyout.
- **Don't** joke inside grief. When Teo's remains come up, the satire leaves the room.
- **Don't** make June a punchline or put her in danger.
- **Don't** do cartel villains, a wise elder mystic, a "the land remembers" speech, or the patriarch's deathbed
  monologue.

### 8.6 Banned clichés and overused words

**Genre and plot clichés:**

- branding a man; "the train station"
- a grizzled-foreman speech about the land; "this is what we do"; "family is everything" played straight
- a gold digger played straight; slow-motion horses at golden hour
- an oil gusher showering a celebrating crowd
- a deathbed speech; a will reading where a lawyer reads aloud to the whole family; a cartel subplot
- "you're not my son"; a dream sequence, or any "it was only a dream"
- an evil activist; a noble savage; a hooker with a heart of gold; a sheriff in sunglasses saying "not in my county"
- Yellowstone, Dutton or Dallas references within the fiction, and no quoting *Chinatown* (we know it exists)

**Prose clichés and words to avoid:**

- "a beat" (in prose), "let out a breath he didn't know he was holding", "the silence was deafening", "little did
  he know"
- "his jaw tightened", "she bit her lip", smirk / smirked, "chuckled darkly", orbs, "piercing eyes", smoldering,
  sultry
- "electric" (once per season, maximum), palpable, visceral, tapestry, testament to, delve, nestled, "an air of",
  unspoken, "a dance" as a metaphor, game-changer, ozone
- "God's country", "cowboy up" (unless mocking), "big sky", "something shifted", "in that moment", "time stood
  still", "It's complicated"

### 8.7 Paloma rules (repeat)

1. Never write her interior thoughts.
2. Never put her alone in a cutaway.
3. She never lies to Whit about her feelings.
4. Keep her tells consistent: stillness, the red chip, Spanish to the mare, refusing to enter the chapel during events.
5. No dossier line about her may go beyond the current reveal layer (§3.5 table).
