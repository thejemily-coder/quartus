# PART I — THE SHOW

## 1. Premise

Fall 2009. Jack Costigan, eighteen, drives two thousand four hundred miles from Youngstown, Ohio, in his laid-off
father's truck to a beach-town public university so good and so godless that his parish priest called it "Babylon with
a surf report." He has come to study philosophy under the famous atheist who once wrote him a one-line postcard, and he
has come carrying a paperback, a confirmation medal, a pack of Marlboro Reds he is quitting, and a secret that a member
of his family is paying for by the day.

On his first night he meets Magdalena Broussard on the back porch of a party neither of them wanted to attend. She is
from New Orleans, she is quicker than he is, she is taken, and she is carrying something too.

Over four years and into the lives after, they will argue about Aquinas and Augustine, about Ted Kennedy and Flannery
O'Connor, about whether you love the world by leaving it or by walking straight into it. They will love other people who
deserve better than to be second. They will be the only two people left standing in a room more than once. And they will
slowly discover that the girl who died at Marisol Point the spring before they arrived is the hinge on which nearly
everyone they love has been quietly turning.

## 2. The thematic spine: the hard saying

John 6. Jesus tells a crowd that has just been fed that he is the bread of life, that unless they eat his flesh and drink
his blood they have no life in them. It is the most scandalous thing he ever says, and he does not soften it. "This is a
hard saying; who can listen to it?" (6:60). "After this many of his disciples drew back and no longer went about with
him" (6:66). Jesus turns to the Twelve: "Do you also wish to go away?" Peter: "Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the
words of eternal life" (6:67–68).

Everything in the show rhymes with that exchange.

- **Love that costs.** Every real love in the show is priced. Danny Costigan pays for Jack's future in years. Frank pays
  for it in a choice he cannot confess. Fr. Osei pays for his vows in silence. Laney pays for Lena's friendship by being
  seen. Julian pays for loving Lena by being asked to stand outside a door he cannot open. Jack and Lena pay for each
  other in waiting.
- **Who stays when others leave.** Wes leaves the Church. Hale left it at seventeen and has been standing outside it in
  the rain ever since. Theo runs. Kevin is told, from two directions, that he must leave something. The show watches who
  stays — at a bedside, in a pew, on a stairwell, on a cliff — and it never pretends staying is easy or that leaving is
  stupid. Peter stays not because he understands but because there is nowhere else to go. That is the show's idea of
  faith: not a feeling, a refusal to leave.
- **The Eucharist as the show's secret center.** Bread that is a body. The ordinary made unbearable. Jack and Lena's
  restraint is, underneath, Eucharistic: the body is not disposable because God hid himself in one. The show never says
  that out loud in narration. Fr. Osei may say it once a season. The rest is in the images: bread, hands, mouths, hunger,
  fasting, the meal after the funeral.
- **Lip service vs. the heart** (the pilot's Gospel, Mark 7): the show's moral epistemology. What comes out of a person
  defiles. Every character performs; the question is what is underneath.

## 3. Tone

*Warmth first, knife second.* Gilmore Girls in the pace and the banter, Hart of Dixie in the sweetness, Greek in the
houses, Task and Strike in the patience of the mystery, Red Dead in the moral weight of one good bad man, The Last of Us
in the way love is the thing that damns and saves. Euphoria only in honesty about bodies and drugs, never in glamour.

Rule of thumb: **three laughs before every cut.** A dark scene earns its darkness by following warmth. A devastating
episode still has a scene where someone eats pie and is ridiculous.

## 4. Style guide

### 4.1 POV and tense
- **Close third, present tense, Jack only.** "Jack watches her cross the porch." The camera never leaves Jack. No
  cutaways, no "meanwhile," no dreams that show what Jack cannot know (a dream may show only what Jack fears).
- Narration knows what Jack knows and sees what Jack sees. It may infer ("something in her face says she has done this
  before"); it may never assert another person's thoughts as fact.
- Jack's interiority is allowed and important, but **dramatized**, not essayed. One sentence of thought, then action.
- The narration's intelligence is Jack's intelligence: a well-read nineteen-year-old from the Rust Belt. It can quote
  Chesterton; it should also notice the price of the gas.

### 4.2 Prose register
- **Literary, warm, funny, sensory. A TV hour on the page.** Scenes are built like television scenes: an establishing
  image (the chyron does the place and time, the first sentence does the light, the weather, the smell), a turn, an exit.
- **Specific nouns.** Not "a drink" — a red Solo cup of Natty Light gone warm. Not "a car" — a 1999 Chevy Silverado with
  rust blooming over the rear wheel wells like a rash. Not "music" — whatever is actually playing.
- **Sensory anchors at Marisol:** eucalyptus (menthol, cat-pee, rain), kelp at low tide, sunscreen, fog that beads on
  wool, the 2 a.m. train horn, sea lions barking off the wharf, jasmine on the Las Olas fences, the sweet chemical smell
  of the Delgado laundry room. **At Youngstown:** wet leaves, the cold metal smell of the garage, Nonna's gravy, Folgers,
  diesel, snow that is gray by noon. **At New Orleans:** sweet olive, boiled crab, rain on hot asphalt, mold, chicory.
- **Sentence music:** vary length. Allow a long, accumulating sentence when Jack is overwhelmed; then a short one.
- **Paragraphs:** dialogue lives inside prose, novel-style. One speaker per paragraph unless it's a rapid-fire exchange
  that reads better stacked.
- **Banned:** the tic registry in `room.md`. Also: "orbs," "padded," "smirked" more than once an episode, "gaze" as a
  verb, "electric" for touch, "a jolt," "time stood still," "the world fell away," "let out a breath he didn't know he
  was holding." No eyes that "darken with desire." No "core."

### 4.3 Dialogue rules
- **Everyone has their own funny.** See voices in Part II. Jack is wry and allusive with Rust Belt bluntness; Lena is
  fast, Southern, combative, theatrical and suddenly tender; Theo is epigrammatic and luxurious; Laney is pageant-Texan
  with filthy timing; Margot is deadpan and polysyllabic; Kevin is dad-joke sunny with a scalpel under it; Osei is
  proverbial and mischievous; Hale is Oxbridge cut-glass with gutter vocabulary.
- **Subtext over text.** People rarely say what they mean when it matters. They talk about the pie. Nobody says "I'm
  scared." They say "Is it cold in here or is it Ohio."
- **2009 mouths.** No therapy-speak ("boundaries," "triggered," "valid," "unpack," "toxic," "lived experience") except
  Margot, who uses academic jargon deliberately and is mocked for it. No "lowkey," "vibe check," "it's giving," "slay,"
  "rizz," "ghosting" (as a verb), "situationship." Era slang is allowed and sparing: "epic fail," "that's what she said,"
  "FML," "sexiled," "hella" (NorCal kids only — Kevin, Julian), "legit," "creeper," "facebook stalking," "the Twilight
  thing," "Team Edward." Jack says "wicked" never; he's from Ohio. Jack says "pop," not "soda." Lena says "y'all," "cher,"
  "making groceries," "neutral ground," "where y'at." Laney says "y'all" too, but Dallas, not New Orleans: "fixin' to."
- **Names:** Lena calls him "Costigan" when fighting, "Jack" when it matters, "Youngstown" when flirting, and once a
  season, "John." Jack calls her "Broussard," "Lena," and, rarely, "Magdalena" — which she allows only from him and only
  when he means it. Theo calls Jack "Costigan," "Father Jack," "my moral compass." People call Theo "Theodore" when he's
  in trouble.
- **Speeches are rare and earned.** One real speech per episode, maximum, usually in the duel or the climax.

### 4.4 How heat is written: restraint is the fire
- The heat is in the **near**: the inch, the almost, the hand that does not move, the breath on a neck in a crowded
  room, the door that stays shut. Proximity, not contact. Specific bodies, specific details: the freckle at the hinge of
  her jaw, the cold of the medal against her collarbone, the way she pushes her sleeves up when she's about to win, the
  stubble he hasn't dealt with, the smell of smoke he can't get out of his jacket.
- **Withholding is active.** When Jack doesn't kiss her, the prose must make the not-kissing an event: show the cost.
- **Desire is good.** Never write desire as dirty. The Catholic claim is that desire is so good it's worth ordering. Jack
  wants her — the prose says so plainly. The restraint is a choice made by a man who wants, not a man who doesn't.
- **When sex is on the page** (other characters, other couples, and later possibly Jack and Lena per player choices):
  frank, literary, adult, specific to the people. Sensation and emotion, the comedy of bodies, the vulnerability, the
  morning after, the loneliness of the wrong bed. Never pornographic mechanics. Never glamorized and never punished by
  the narration; consequences come from character, not from the author's thumb.
- Everyone in sexual content is **18 or older** (see §9 birthdays).

### 4.5 How faith is written: true, and hard
- **Flannery O'Connor, not a pamphlet.** Grace is real and it is violent, inconvenient, funny, and usually arrives
  through the wrong person. Sin has weight; so does mercy.
- **The sacraments work.** Confession actually absolves. The Eucharist is actually Christ. The narration never winks.
  But the narration never *proves* it either: Jack experiences grace the way people do — as peace he can't account for,
  as a sentence that arrives when he needed it, as the strength to not do the thing.
- **Prayer on the page:** short, real, unpolished. Jack prays like a man talking to someone in the next room. He uses the
  Hail Mary when he can't think, the Jesus Prayer when he's scared, and on bad nights the St. Michael prayer like a
  bouncer's checklist. Lena prays the Memorare and argues with God out loud.
- **Liturgy is accurate:** the real readings for the real Sunday, the real feast days, the real colors. Homilies are
  excerpted, never transcribed whole: two or three sentences that land.
- **Catholics are not the good guys.** Newman has hypocrites, bores, saints, fools, a gossip, a missionary who means it
  and a missionary who's faking it. The faith is true; the faithful are people.
- **The critics get their best arguments.** Margot is often right. Hale is often right. Julian is often the most
  Christ-like person in the room and doesn't believe a word of it. The story never sneers at belief or unbelief.

### 4.6 How the arguments are written
- **Both brilliant, both real.** Real philosophy, real texts, real citations: Aquinas (*ST* I-II q.94 on natural law,
  q.109–114 on grace; *Summa contra Gentiles*), Augustine (*Confessions* VIII, *City of God* XIV, *Enchiridion*,
  "Dilige, et quod vis fac" — *Tractates on 1 John* 7.8), Pascal (*Pensées*, the wager, "cela vous abêtira"), Aristotle
  (*NE* II on habituation), Kierkegaard (*Attack upon "Christendom"*), Newman (*Grammar of Assent*, *Apologia*),
  Anscombe ("Modern Moral Philosophy"), MacIntyre (*After Virtue*), Bernard Williams ("Moral Luck," *Ethics and the
  Limits of Philosophy*), Nietzsche (*Genealogy*), Hume (*Dialogues*), Russell, Mackie (*The Miracle of Theism*), Dennett,
  Hitchens (*god is not Great*, 2007), Simone de Beauvoir (*The Second Sex*), Judith Butler, bell hooks, Mary Daly,
  Dorothy Day, Chesterton (*Orthodoxy*, *The Everlasting Man*), C.S. Lewis, Lewis vs. Tolkien on allegory, O'Connor
  (*Mystery and Manners*, "A Good Man Is Hard to Find," "Revelation," "Parker's Back"), Waugh (*Brideshead*), Greene
  (*The Power and the Glory*, *The End of the Affair*), Percy (*The Moviegoer*, *Love in the Ruins*), Dostoevsky (the
  Grand Inquisitor; Zosima), Weil, Benedict XVI (*Deus Caritas Est*, *Spe Salvi*, *Caritas in Veritate* — July 7, 2009),
  John Paul II (*Theology of the Body*, *Veritatis Splendor*, *Evangelium Vitae*).
- **Quote accurately or paraphrase honestly.** If a writer isn't sure of a quote, paraphrase it in-character ("Pascal says
  something like…").
- **Lena wins about as often as Jack does.** Across a season, tally. Lena's default mode is Thomist-with-a-machete: she
  trusts reason, nature, the body, form, the visible Church; she is merciful toward persons and merciless toward
  arguments. Jack's default mode is Augustinian-with-a-smoker's-cough: he trusts grace, distrusts nature (his own
  especially), feels the fall in his bones, is merciful toward arguments and hard on himself. **They swap positions when
  the facts get personal** — that's where the best duels live.
- **The arguments are flirtation and also real.** Every duel has a moment where it stops being a game. Winning isn't
  always good. Conceding the right point at the right moment is the most romantic thing either of them can do.
- **Politics (reconciled with the brief's map after S1E01):** both are *nearly* politically homeless, both deeply
  pro-life, both convinced that both parties betray the Church, and each thinks the other is wrong for leaning where they
  lean. **Lena** is a Catholic Worker type (Dorothy Day), a pro-life Louisiana Democrat in her bones — union fishermen,
  Charity Hospital, the levees, the death penalty (against), immigrants (for), Katrina as proof the state abandons the
  poor — and ferocious about abortion and euthanasia. **Jack** is more comfortable on the right: a law-and-order Rust Belt
  conservative raised in a Kennedy-Democrat union house (Frank) who broke with it at the kitchen table. Labor is still in
  his blood (his father's layoff is personal) and he distrusts the market's mercy as much as the government's, but his
  instincts run to order, crime and punishment and the culture war, and he votes like it. 🔒 The irony the show never
  states: the law-and-order boy is the reason his brother is in prison. **Over the run (brief):** Lena's struggle deepens
  as the Democrats harden on abortion and as the LGBT movement shifts, in her view, from human dignity to remaking sex and
  the family; Jack keeps hearing racial overtones inside what he considers reasonable disagreements with Obama; the Trump
  years are very hard on Jack (immigration enforcement turning, as he sees it, into dehumanization); Dobbs (2022) is joy
  for both, then ash (mailed pills backed by Republicans too, IVF's expansion); both grow disgusted with the whole system.
  These are convictions written from inside; Margot, Theo, Hale, Kevin, Julian and Yusuf get their best arguments. They
  fight about the health-care bill and Stupak, Notre Dame and Obama (May 2009), Ted Kennedy's funeral, the death penalty,
  immigration, Traficant (Jack: a crook; Frank: *our* crook), and whether the bishops are cowards or courageous about the
  abuse scandal (they agree the bishops failed; they disagree about what that means for obedience).

### 4.7 How comedy works
- **Character comedy, never mockery.** The funniest people are funny on purpose (Lena, Theo, Laney, Margot) and the
  second-funniest are funny by accident (Priya's laminated notes, Joey Abruzzo's volume, Doug Pruitt existing).
- **Running jokes pay off** (see Part IV §26). The scar story. The cigarette reliquary. Margot's whiteboard. The 3 a.m.
  fire alarm. The Anchor's pie ratings.
- **Banter has rhythm**: set, turn, topper — then break the pattern; let someone fail to land one.
- **Jack is funny in a way that keeps people at arm's length.** The show eventually notices that.
- **The sacred is never the butt of the joke**; Catholics are, often.

### 4.8 Content standards (per the brief)
- Sex, desire, bodies, drug use, violence, addiction, overdose, death and grief: frank and literary, HBO-level, always
  in service of character and emotion. Morning-afters, bad sex, good sex, funny sex, the loneliness of a hookup. Never
  pornographic mechanics.
- **Everyone in any sexual content is 18+.** Every freshman in the show is 18 or older at move-in (see §9).
- **Sexual assault:** may be a storyline; if used, it is centered on the survivor's experience and aftermath, never
  titillating, never a twist. **Currently no assault storyline is scheduled.** If one is introduced, it requires a
  Showrunner note and a revision-log entry.
- Addiction and overdose: real, unglamorous. Someone may not make it (see §19, Death register).
- **Hard lines:** the narrative never mocks the faith. No cheap villains, no secular strawmen. **Jack and Lena never
  die.**
- Real people (politicians, clergy, journalists, celebrities) appear only through the news, TV, the radio, the paper,
  and characters' opinions about public events. Never invent private conduct for a real person. Never assert, in-world,
  any fact about a real private event that the public record does not support.

## 6. JACK COSTIGAN (the player)

**Full name:** John Anthony Costigan. Confirmation name Michael. Born **March 19, 1991** (St. Joseph's Day) — 18 at
move-in. Youngstown, Ohio (South Side, Brownlee Woods). Cardinal Mooney High School '09: point guard, Latin Club, the kid
who argued with the theology teacher and then served the 6:30 a.m. Mass. Philosophy major. Delgado Hall 412.

**Public face.** The funniest guy on the floor. Charming, quick, a little dangerous, generous with his last twenty,
devastating at 2 a.m. arguments, holds a door, holds a grudge, holds his liquor less well than he thinks. The guy who
goes to Mass hungover and the guy who goes. Smokes when stressed or drinking; is quitting. Reads constantly; the
paperback in his back pocket has margins full of fights with the author.

**Private truth. 🔒** He believes he is not a good man and that everyone who loves him is being conned. He has a reason.
(See §17, Jack's secret.) His charm is a way of paying in advance. His faith is real — not inherited, *chosen*, at a
specific moment in a specific place (a holding cell bench, 🔒 §17) — and it is the one thing he's never performed. He
came to Marisol to fight Adrian Hale because Hale's book nearly took that faith from him at sixteen, and because a part
of him wants to lose.

**Wound.** 🔒 His brother is in prison for something Jack did.

**Want vs. need.** *Want:* to be worth what it cost. *Need:* to accept that he can't be, and that grace is not a payment
plan.

**Convictions (brief).** Law-and-order Rust Belt conservative (§4.6). **Loves Benedict XVI** — the theologian-pope,
Regensburg, *Spe Salvi*, *Caritas in Veritate*, the liturgy; carries *Spe Salvi* with a Benedict holy card (S1E02); the
resignation (Feb 11, 2013) lands hard. **Struggles with Francis** (says, repeatedly, that Francis has held the line on
doctrine; hates how he treats the conservative cardinals Jack admires). **Adores Leo XIV** (2025). Gets deep into **Bishop
Barron / Word on Fire** from the *Catholicism* series (2011) and later defends him as doing the best he can.

**Voice.** Wry, allusive, Rust Belt blunt. Says "pop," "gumband" (rubber band; Youngstown), "jagoff" (rarely, with
affection), "yinz" never (that's Pittsburgh; he'll correct you). Quotes Chesterton, Springsteen, the Baltimore Catechism,
and his nonna, sometimes in one sentence. Jokes as deflection — when someone gets close, he gets funnier. Under real
pressure, he gets very quiet and very plain. Never says "I'm fine"; says "I'm good," which is worse.
- *"I'm not quitting. I'm tapering. Like a saint, but with Marlboros."*
- *"In Youngstown we don't say 'I love you.' We say 'Text me when you get there,' and then we don't sleep."*
- *"Chesterton says the Church is a house with a hundred gates. I came in through the one with the smoking section."*
- *"I believe in the resurrection of the body. I just don't trust mine."*
- Quiet register: *"Okay. Okay. Tell me what you need."*

**Look (brief).** 6'0", black hair that needs cutting, pale skin, blue eyes, a nose broken once in a CYO basketball
fight (true: St. Christine's vs. St. Brigid's, eighth grade, he started it), stubble by noon. St. Michael medal from his
confirmation. A scar through his left eyebrow whose story he tells differently every time. A battered paperback in his
back pocket. Marlboro Reds. Wardrobe: his brother's old Carhartt jacket (too big in the shoulders — 🔒 Danny's), flannel,
a gray Cardinal Mooney Cardinals hoodie, one good navy blazer from the Goodwill on Market Street for Mass and Hale.
**Phone:** a Motorola RAZR V3 with a cracked hinge, Gina's hand-me-down; T9; he texts like a telegram.

**Arc (full run).** From penance-as-performance to mercy received. S1: the charming rival who can't be honest. S2: the
pull of the world (Sol); the knowledge of Theo's secret lands on him. S3: the tryer (Paige); holding Theo's secret while
asking everyone else for truth. S4: Lena, at last, and the lie that blows it up. S5: exile, Wes's funeral, the seal on
trial. S6: repair. S7–S8: the truth about Danny, told to the people who are owed it; Benedict resigns, Francis arrives;
graduation. Adulthood: he goes home. 🔒 Endgame Jack becomes a high-school theology and philosophy teacher at Cardinal
Mooney, and later a prison chaplaincy volunteer at Trumbull Correctional. He never becomes a priest; the show flirts with
it once (S6) and the flirtation is real.

## 7. MAGDALENA "LENA" BROUSSARD

**Full name:** Magdalena Thérèse Broussard. Born **July 22, 1991** (feast of St. Mary Magdalene) — 18 at move-in. New
Orleans, Mid-City (Bayou St. John), the house on N. Dupre Street that took four and a half feet of water in 2005.
Academy of the Sacred Heart ("the Rosary") '09: debate captain, state champion in Lincoln-Douglas, Latin and Greek.
Classics/philosophy. Delgado Hall 217.

**Public face.** Incandescent. The girl who wins the argument, the room, the parking dispute. Funny, fast, theatrical,
generous, impossible to out-talk. Devout without apology: daily Mass when she can manage it, mantilla sometimes, a rosary
in her boot. Loves a party and loves leaving one early. Cooks for the whole floor on Sundays (red beans, a gumbo when she
can get okra). Has a boyfriend back home — everyone on the floor knows by the end of week one, because she says so.

**Private truth. 🔒** She is holding three secrets so tightly she has forgotten what her hands feel like open (§18):
what she did in April 2009 at Marisol; what happened to her grandmother at Memorial Medical Center; and how bad things
have quietly become with Wes. Her fierceness is real and it is also armor. She touches her grandmother's Miraculous Medal
when she's thinking — and when she's lying. She doesn't know she does the second. (The player knows the tell — it's in the
brief — but Jack doesn't, and the player never knows *what* she's lying about. Every touch must be honest bookkeeping.)

**Wound.** 🔒 Katrina, and the question of what her mother did or didn't do on the fourth day. Also: the dead girl whose
bed she slept beside.

**Convictions (brief).** Catholic Worker, Dorothy Day, pro-life Democrat (§4.6). **Respects Benedict XVI's mind and office;
not enthralled** ("I'd follow him into a burning building. I wouldn't put him on my wall"). **Adores Francis** — the
peripheries, mercy, the poor, *Laudato Si'*, the field hospital. **Adores Leo XIV.** Likes Barron quite a bit; grows
dissatisfied with him in the Trump years.

**Want vs. need.** *Want:* to be right — to have argued every case so well that nothing can be her fault. *Need:* to be
forgiven for something she can't argue her way out of, and to let someone carry it with her.

**Voice.** Fast, Southern, combative, theatrical, then suddenly — without warning — tender and plain. Long sentences that
build to a verdict. Uses "cher" (to Laney and to children), "y'all," "making groceries," "neutral ground," "lagniappe,"
"Lord have mercy" (sincerely and sarcastically). Swears creatively and Catholic-ly: "Sweet suffering Saint Sebastian."
Cites Aquinas like a lawyer cites precedent. Narrates her own outrage in the third person when very angry ("Oh, Magdalena
is *going* to say something"). When hurt she goes formal and polite, which is terrifying.
- *"I'm not against fun. I'm against fun that wants your car keys and your name."*
- *"You're carrying a New Orleans novel to California. That's either a pickup line or a cry for help, and I need to know
  which before I decide how to be disappointed."*
- *"Aquinas says grace perfects nature. He doesn't say it replaces it. You keep trying to skip to the part where God
  does your homework."*
- *"My grandmother's dead and she still makes me wear it."*
- Plain register: *"Don't. Don't be funny right now. Just stay."*

**Look (brief).** Sun-streaked honey-brown waves, golden skin, warm amber-brown eyes; classic Gulf Coast beauty. About
5'5", soft and curvy, at peace with her body, neither hiding nor flaunting it; warmth in every line. Grandmother's worn
gold Miraculous Medal on a thin chain. Thrifted sundresses with beat-up cowboy boots (brown Justins, resoled twice) or
Keds; a cardigan (oatmeal, holes at the elbows) when the fog rolls in. Sometimes veils at Mass in a black lace mantilla.
**Phone:** LG enV2 with the keyboard; texts in complete sentences with punctuation, which Jack finds unbearable and hot.

**Arc (full run).** From advocate to penitent to witness. S1: the rival who won't let anyone see her; Wes unravels; she
loses him. S2: rebound and recovery; Julian begins; Mardi Gras and the Saints; her public debate with Hale. S3: Julian,
the tryer; Laney's overdose; the Memorial question opened with her mother. S4: Jack — and the confession that costs her
him. S5: Wes's death; the hardest season. S6: forgiveness given and received. S7–S8: the line, the election, the
resignation, the new pope, graduation, a ring. Adulthood: 🔒 Tulane Law, then the Orleans Public Defenders — she argues
for people no one else will. Her mother's secret is finally spoken at Lena's own wedding rehearsal dinner (adult seasons).

## 8. THE CORE ENSEMBLE

### 8.1 THEO VANCE
**Full name:** Theodore Ashby Vance. Born **November 3, 1990** — 18 at move-in, 19 in S1E13. Brentwood (father) /
La Cañada Flintridge (mother), Los Angeles. Harvard-Westlake '09 (barely). Undeclared; secretly a film obsessive. Delgado
Hall 412.

**Family.** Father **Richard Vance**, 56, president of production at Meridian Pictures (fictional), a producer credit on a
Best Picture winner (fictional, 2003), on his second marriage (to Brynn, 31, a former development exec). Mother **Caroline
Ashby Vance**, 50, a former actress (one season of a 1980s nighttime soap), Pasadena old money, lapsed Catholic who had
Theo baptized "for Grandmother Ashby," now on her third wellness guru and fourth Pomeranian. Divorced 2004. Sister
**Poppy Vance**, 14, the only person Theo answers the phone for on the first ring.
**Richard's Malibu house (S1E03):** glass and teak on the sand at Broad Beach, west of Zuma, below the PCH; a pool on a
deck over the beach stairs; a screening room nobody uses; a chef-stocked refrigerator with labeled containers nobody is
allowed to eat; the housekeeper (Inés, weekdays) is off for Labor Day. **The Oscar is real** (the producer statuette for the
fictional 2003 Best Picture, *The Long Field*), kept in the downstairs powder room as a joke Richard tells at dinner parties:

### 10.1 WES ARCENEAUX — Lena's boyfriend at the opening 🔒
**Full name:** Wesley Joseph Arceneaux. Born **June 14, 1989** — 20. New Orleans (Gentilly). Jesuit High '07. Junior at
Tulane, civil engineering ("I'm going to build the walls right"). Lena's older brother Luc's best friend since T-ball.

**Who he is.** During Katrina, at sixteen, Wes took his uncle's aluminum flatboat out on the third day and ran it through
Gentilly and the edge of the Ninth Ward for four days, pulling people off roofs and out of attics. He is a neighborhood
legend. He carried Lena's twin siblings out of the second floor of a neighbor's house. He has been in love with Lena
since she was fifteen, and he waited until she was seventeen to say so. They've dated since her senior-year Mardi Gras
(February 2009). They are "holding the line" together — his idea as much as hers. He's funny (a gentle, goofy, Saints-
obsessed humor), kind, devout in the way of a Jesuit boy: he can do the Ignatian Examen and the Who Dat chant in one
breath. He's the kind of boyfriend parents pray for.

**The unraveling (sad, not expected).** He is not cheating. He is not a cad. **He is losing God.** On the fourth day of the
boat, behind a house on Pauger Street, he heard knocking under a roofline — a woman in an attic — and couldn't get the
axe through the rafters before the water rose and his uncle made him go to the next house; when they came back in the
morning the knocking had stopped. He has never told anyone: not his uncle, not Lena, not a priest. Four years later,
studying the Army Corps of Engineers' failures in a Tulane classroom, he has begun to believe that the water was just
water and the knocking was just a woman and there was no one listening to either of them. He stops going to Mass in
September. He doesn't tell Lena. He drinks a little more each week. He keeps calling her every night at 10 Central / 8
Pacific, sweet and funny, and she keeps not noticing, because she doesn't want to.

**Jack and Wes.** Wes visits Marisol Columbus Day weekend (S1E09). Jack expects to hate him and doesn't. They end up on the
beach at 2 a.m. with a bottle of Jameson; Wes tells Jack the attic story — the one he's never told Lena — because Jack is
a stranger, and you can tell a stranger. Wes says: "Take care of her. She thinks she's the one doing the carrying." Jack
now knows something about Lena's boyfriend that Lena doesn't. That knowledge is a weight on him for the rest of the season.

**The end (S1E21, Dec 12, 2009, Our Lady of Guadalupe).** On the east stairwell phone, Wes breaks up with her — gently,
devastatingly. Not because there's someone else. Because he can't kneel anymore, and he won't let her marry a man who
stands at the back of the church at their children's baptisms with his hands in his pockets. "You'd do it. You'd marry
me anyway. That's why I can't let you." She argues — she is impossible to out-talk — and she loses, because you cannot
argue someone into faith, and she knows it, and that is the worst thing she learns all year. Jack sits on the stairs
with her until dawn and does not kiss her.

**After.** Lena keeps praying for him. Wes appears at Mardi Gras 2010 (S2) — sober-ish, kind, broken. 🔒 **He dies in
August 2011 (S5 opener)**, on the sixth anniversary of the storm, of an accidental overdose of oxycodone and alcohol. Jack
goes to New Orleans for the funeral. It's the hinge of the reconciliation. *(Death register: reconsider before S5 based
on the path; the Showrunner may instead let Wes live and return to the Church in adulthood.)*

**Voice.** Easy, slow, goofy, Gentilly-Y'at ("where y'at," "dawlin'," "yeah you right"). Talks about the Saints like
scripture. Underneath, long pauses.
- *"You're the Ohio guy. She said you were a jagoff. That means she likes you, I've known her since she had a retainer."*

### 10.2 SOL ARRIAGADA — Jack's magnetic temptation 🔒

**What she wants from Jack.** Precisely the thing he won't give her, and to understand why he won't. She is not trying to

## 15. The elision rule (how close third keeps Jack's secret honestly)

Jack is the POV and the player *is* Jack, so the narration must never lie about what Jack did. It may **elide**: it can
decline to narrate a memory. Jack thinks about Danny constantly; the narration says so. It shows Jack flinching from the
scar story, smoking when Danny's name comes up, sending money orders to a "D. Kostelnik" (S1E05, a detail with no
explanation), writing letters he doesn't send. It never says "Danny did it" in Jack's voice; it never says "Jack did it"
until the reveal. When the truth comes out (S2E21), every earlier scene must read as honest in retrospect: the player
should feel *Jack was carrying this the whole time and I was carrying it with him without knowing.*
- Allowed: "Jack doesn't think about that night. He's good at it."
- Not allowed: "Jack has never hurt anyone."

## 17. JACK'S SECRET (full)

**What happened:** §16. Mikey Kostelnik lived. He has a traumatic brain injury. Struthers PD found Danny with blood on his
shirt and Jack's scholarship-track future on the bench in the back of the cruiser. Danny had a prior (a 2006 bar fight,
misdemeanor assault) and was on probation; the Mahoning County prosecutor charged felonious assault; Danny took a plea
for five years in January 2008. At the station at 4 a.m., Jack, sixteen, tried once to tell a detective the truth; Frank
walked in and took him out of the room by the back of his neck. On the bench in the hall, Jack prayed for the first time
in his life like someone was there. He has been praying like that ever since. **His faith was born the night he became a
coward.** That's the knot.

**Why it matters to the show:** Jack spends seasons judging Theo's silence — and Lena's — while living inside his own. The
central mystery is a mirror: everyone who loves Theo will be asked what Jack's family already chose. Hale's specialty is
*moral luck* (Williams, Nagel): the idea that we are judged for outcomes we didn't control. Jack threw one punch in
defense of a girl; Mikey hit a cinderblock. Theo grabbed a girl to save her; she fell. Hale's seminar will put the
question to Jack in the abstract in S1E05 and he will argue it like his life depends on it, and the player won't know
why yet.

**The money orders.** Jack sends $20–$60 monthly to Donna Kostelnik, unsigned. Shown without explanation from S1E05.

**The clock.** 🔒 Jack is aware, vaguely and with dread, that there are time limits on prosecutions, and that if he does
nothing long enough, nothing can be done. *Writers: never specify the law on the page. The dread is moral, not legal.*
The show's position: he will tell, and the telling (S7, Fall 2012, to Mikey's mother first, then to the Mahoning County
prosecutor) costs him; what the law does about it is less important than what Donna Kostelnik does — 🔒 she slams the

## 20. THE CENTRAL MYSTERY — "THE POINT" 🔒

The player chose "surprise me completely." This is the spine that will entangle the ensemble from the pilot through at
least S5, with consequences through the full run.

### 20.1 The surface (what Marisol believes)
At about 6:10 a.m. on **Sunday, April 26, 2009** (the Third Sunday of Easter), two surfers paddling out at the Point break
found the body of **Nora Grace Calloway**, 19, a sophomore, on the rocks below the old Point Marisol Light. The coroner
found a BAC of 0.09 and a therapeutic level of alprazolam. Her phone was in her jeans, destroyed. Marisol PD (Det. Sgt.
Ray Ochoa) ruled it an accidental fall: she was known to sit on the seaward side of the low wall at night. The campus held
a vigil. St. Brendan's held a memorial Mass. A white wooden cross stands at the wall, and every Sunday before dawn
someone leaves fresh flowers there; nobody knows who. Privately, many — including her parents' parish in Selma, the
detective, Julian, and later Margot — believe it was suicide.

### 20.2 Who she was
Nora Grace Calloway, born **September 8, 1989** (the Nativity of Mary) in Selma, California. Father Tom, a raisin farmer;
mother Rosa (Gutiérrez), a school-district bookkeeper; two younger brothers (Mateo, 15; Sam, 12). Philosophy major, Music
minor, Newman choir soprano. Funny, generous, intense, devout, doubting, brave. Freshman year in Delgado 222 with Priya;
sophomore year in Serra 3B with Sloane. She wore a Levi's jean jacket with a **Fresno Fair pin** on the collar and an
embroidered **Our Lady of Guadalupe patch** sewn on the back by her grandmother. She sang with Julian at the Point wall.
She sent Hale a rebuttal in high school and got his postcard. She took his seminar. She fell in love with his TA.

### 20.3 The truth of the night (timeline)
- **Oct 2008–Apr 2009:** Nora and Evan Marsh, secretly together.
- **Sat Apr 25, 2:14 p.m.:** Walgreens on Mission Street: pregnancy test. Positive (about 8 weeks).
- **6:00–8:00 p.m.:** Newman admit dinner (Nora hosts Lena).
- **~8:30 p.m.:** Nora texts Evan the news. He calls; they fight — he panics, says "we can't," says "there are options."
- **10:30 p.m.:** Nora goes to the Lighthouse party (the last house on Seacliff Road, rented by senior surfers). Julian is
  there with his guitar; they sing one song on the stairs ("Skinny Love"). Rafi Ochoa, a high-school senior then, is
  selling. Nora buys two Xanax bars from him: she hasn't slept in a week. She takes one with a beer. Sloane sees her leave
  at ~10:50, crying.
- **Also at the Lighthouse:** Theo Vance, 18, an admit, crashing with an LA friend's older brother who lives there. Around
  10:45 he goes down the bluff stairs to the beach to smoke and meets **Kevin Tran**, 18, an admit, who wandered out of
  the party because he couldn't breathe. They talk for an hour. They kiss. Kevin leaves ~11:50 for his host's room in
  Serra Hall, shaking with joy and terror. Theo stays, does two lines of cocaine alone on the stairs, and walks out along
  the bluff toward the light.
- **11:15 p.m.:** Nora back at Serra; the bathroom; the Memorare. **11:40:** "Cover for me, Magdalena." **11:45:** Lena sees
  the test.
- **11:52 p.m.:** On the Point path, Nora calls Hale. Forty-one seconds. *"Go home, Nora. You're drunk and you're boring
  me."*
- **12:05 a.m.:** Nora texts Evan: *"I'm at the Point. Please come. I'm keeping it. I want to keep it. I want you to want
  it."* She sits on the seaward side of the wall, legs over the drop, as she has done a hundred times, and prays aloud.
- **~12:25 a.m.:** Theo, high, comes up the path in the dark and sees a girl on the outside of the wall, talking to
  someone who isn't there. He thinks she's about to jump. He runs at her and grabs her from behind by the jacket. She
  startles, twists, her hand slips off the wall; the jacket pulls off her shoulders as she goes; he's left holding it. She
  doesn't scream. He hears her hit. The last words he heard her say were *"…never was it known that anyone who fled to thy
  protection…"*
- **~12:27–12:31:** Theo, in shock, runs. Down the Point path toward Seacliff Road, the jacket clutched to his chest.
- **12:20:** Evan reads the text and drives. **12:31:** at the Point lot his headlights catch a tall blond boy running
  toward Seacliff Road holding something dark. Evan goes to the wall; it's black; he calls her phone (it's under her, in
  the surf, already dead); he waits ten minutes, decides she's left to punish him, drives home. He deletes the text the
  next morning. He keeps a screenshot on his laptop he took at 12:21 to "show her later how scared he'd been." It's still
  there.
- **~12:45 a.m.:** Theo walks to Serra Hall — the only place on campus he knows anyone — and knocks on Kevin's host's door
  (Serra 3F, two doors from Nora's room), soaked to the knees from crossing the creek mouth, palms scraped raw from the
  path, holding the jacket. He's crying. *"You never saw me."* Kevin lets him in; he sleeps on the floor; he's gone at
  dawn. **This is the crying boy Lena heard.**
- **6:10 a.m.:** surfers find her.

### 20.4 The layers of guilt (no villains)
Every person who touched that night believes, privately, that they killed Nora Calloway:
- **Theo** — he did, by trying to save her. The truest guilt and the least culpable intent.
- **Evan** — he said "there are options," didn't answer at 12:05, saw the boy, drove away, lied.
- **Hale** — forty-one seconds.
- **Lena** — she covered for her; she didn't stop her; she hid the test; she didn't look at the crying boy.
- **Rafi** — the Xanax.
- **Kevin** — he let Theo in and never asked.
- **Julian** — he didn't see she was drowning.
- **Sloane** — she let her walk out of the party crying.
- **Priya** — they drifted.
- **Ray Ochoa** — he closed it as an accident while believing it was suicide, out of mercy, and that mercy kept the truth
  buried.

The show's answer, delivered over seasons and never as a speech: everyone did a small wrong thing, the fall was an
accident, and Nora **was not trying to die** — she had chosen to keep her child and was praying the Memorare on the wall
where she always prayed. The proof is two things only a guilty man and a coward kept: Evan's screenshot of the text, and
Theo's memory of the prayer. The truth heals one person completely — Rosa Calloway, her mother, who has believed for two
years that her daughter killed herself and her grandchild. In S5, Rosa receives Communion for the first time since April
2009. That's the payoff the whole mystery is built for.

### 24.4 How the meters bend the route (never break endgame)
- **Endgame is fixed:** they marry. What the player shapes is *how*, *when*, *at what cost*, and *who they are when they
  get there*.
- **High `rel.lena.trust`** shortens the S4 rupture (she forgives sooner; S5 has fewer episodes apart). **Low trust**
  lengthens it into S6 and gives Andrew (S5) real weight.
- **High `rel.lena.heat` with low `rel.lena.trust`**: the relationship becomes about the line, not the person; S4 is
  feverish; the rupture is worse.
- **High `rel.lena.friction`**: more duels, sharper; Lena wins more; the relationship is a fire that needs feeding.
  Friction is not bad — some friction is how they love.
- **`soul.grace`** governs whether Jack goes to confession after a fall, and how fast; **`soul.honesty`** governs when
  he tells Lena the truth about Theo (high honesty → he tells her before Theo surrenders and loses her anyway, but the
  repair is faster); **`soul.restraint`** governs the danger windows and Sol.
- **Lena's other relationships** run regardless of the player; Jack's choices color them (a jealous Jack makes Julian's
  arc sadder; a generous Jack becomes Julian's friend, and Julian's ending is cleaner).
- **Jack's other relationships**: Becca (fixed, short); Sol (player can decline entirely — then S2 is lonelier and his
  resolve dips through Theo instead); Paige (player can decline — then Jack's S3 is about Theo's secret alone, and the
  first kiss comes sooner and worse).

### 24.5 The Ephesians 5 conversation (reserved; brief directive)
- **When:** S7E09 "Subject" (late Oct 2012), not before they are seriously together. It can play as a full duel and define
  the episode.
- **Seeds only before then:** S1E06 Margot reads Eph 5:22–24 at the chastity talk Q&A; the Sunday before the pilot (Aug 23,
  2009) had Eph 5:21–32 as the second reading, so both heard it at home the week they left — a callback either can spring;

- **Texts:** Eph 5:21–33 ("Be subject to one another out of reverence for Christ"; "Husbands, love your wives, as Christ
  loved the church and gave himself up for her"); *Mulieris Dignitatem* §24 (mutual subjection); John Paul II's Theology of
  the Body audiences on Ephesians 5 (1982); *Casti Connubii* (1930); headship as service and sacrifice vs. as authority.
- **What each of them hears (don't assume sides):** 🔒 Jack's father exercised headship once that mattered — he decided,

# PART IV — THE WORLD

## 25. California State University at Marisol

**Where.** A fictional town of 38,000 (plus 24,000 students) on California's Central Coast, on a marine terrace where the
coast bends west, roughly between San Luis Obispo and Santa Barbara. Highway 1 / US-101 runs inland of town; the Amtrak
Pacific Surfliner and the Coast Starlight run along the bluff (the 2:10 a.m. freight horn is the show's clock). Nearest
real airport: Santa Barbara (SBA), fifty-five minutes south; LAX is three hours by car or a Surfliner-and-FlyAway
odyssey. San Francisco: five hours north.

**Climate and light.** Late summer: fog in the morning ("the marine layer"), burning off by eleven; 70s by afternoon; fog
back by dusk; 55–58°F at night. September and October are the warmest, clearest months (Indian summer, Santa Ana days
when the wind turns and the air smells like hot sage and fire). November rains begin. Winter: green hills, storms,
big surf. Monarch butterflies overwinter in the eucalyptus Grove from late October to February.

**Reputation.** Elite public flagship: top-15 public university, Nobel laureates in physics and economics, a ferocious
honors college, a party reputation it hates and earns. Intensely secular and progressive. About 7% of students identify as
practicing Catholics; Newman's Sunday attendance is about 600 across five Masses.

# PART V — THE GAME IN STORY TERMS

## 27. Resolve (hidden; 0–10; both default 7)

Resolve is not willpower in the abstract. It's **the strength to choose the good thing when the easy thing is close.** It
is fed by grace and good company and drained by exhaustion, loneliness, booze and near-misses.

**Jack's resolve — raised by (+1 typical, +2 big):** going to Mass (+1); confession (+2); real prayer chosen by the player
(+1); a night of sleep after a crisis (+1); a good, sober night with friends — Kevin, Margot, Osei's chess (+1); calling
home and it going well (+1); telling the truth when it costs (+1); turning down a drink/drug/cigarette when it's offered
(+1, only when the refusal is a real choice); helping someone (carrying Laney home) (+1).
**Lowered by:** each real drink after the first (−1); cigarettes (−0 alone, −1 with drinking); drugs (−2); loneliness — a
night alone after bad news (−1); a fight with Lena (−1) or with Theo (−1); a charged near-miss with anyone (−1); lying to
someone who trusts him (−1); skipping Mass (−1); bad news from home (−1); sleeping on a couch after being sexiled (−1, a
joke with teeth).

**Lena's resolve (writer-set).** Writers set it via scene `set` at the top of key scenes, based on her canon life: her
prayer life (high baseline), Wes (a slow drain S1), Laney (draining), her grief and secrets (deep drain when stirred —
the Times article, All Souls, the anniversary), exhaustion (finals), alcohol (she drinks moderately; when she drinks to
forget, it's a signal). Track her value in `continuity.md` at the end of every episode.
- **Played (S1E01):** ended at **6** (porch 6, Mass 8, Anchor 6). **Played (S1E02):** 6 → 7 (seminar) → 8 (daily Mass)
  → 7 (the steps) → 6 (Wes misses 8:04; the 11:52 text) → **ends 6.** **S1E03 plan (the breather):** 7 (the road, her
  friends, a kitchen), 8 (Sunday Mass), 8 (she wins the duel; Wes called on time) → **ends 8** (played as planned). **S1E04
  plan:** 5 (s02, the Nativity — 🔒 Nora's birthday) → 5 (seminar) → **4** (the song; the pier — she smokes half his
  cigarette on the lit path) → 5 (the steps; being known a little) → 5 (Wes's 2:04 a.m. party call) → **ends 5** (played as
  planned). **S1E05 plan (recovery):** 6 (Sully's door: game day) → 7 (the win) → 8 (Sunday Mass) → 7 (the duel; 6 on the
  Personal path) → 7 (Bid Day; Danny's call) → **ends 7** (played as planned; not the Personal path). **S1E06 plan:** 7 (the
  alarm; the talk; the cleanup) → **6** (s09, the Pi Lam steps: sober, dismissed by the house, Wes's 9:40 call from somewhere
  loud) → **5** (the lifeguard tower: Wes named, "I've decided not to notice") → 5 (the bench) → **7** (Sunday Mass) → **ends 7.**
  Nothing dangerous: Jack enters at 10.
- Pilot baseline: **7** at move-in; **6** after the party (Laney, the Lighthouse — 🔒 the house itself costs her); **8** after
  Mass; **6** after the diner (the magazine); **6** at the tag (Wes, the article she hasn't read).

**On the page.** Resolve never appears as a number. High resolve: Jack's interior voice is clear, funny, steady; he can
step back and see the moment. Low resolve: sentences get shorter, sensations louder (the smell of her hair, the heat of
the room), the jokes curdle, the prayer comes out as one word or not at all. At ≤3, the narration may admit "he doesn't
want to be good right now" — it never pretends he isn't choosing.

## 28. The soul track (signed axes)

- **`soul.grace` (grace ↔ drift).** High: Jack notices God in things without making a speech about it — the light on the
  water, the bread, the kindness of a stranger; prayer arrives easily; options to pray, bless, forgive appear. Low
  (drift): the world gets flatter and funnier and lonelier; his inner voice gets Hale's cadence; prayer options shrink to
  rote ones; cynical options appear.
- **`soul.honesty` (honesty ↔ performance).** High: Jack's options include saying the plain true thing; his narration
  catches his own lies; candor options unlock. Low: he gets more charming, more performative — more options for wit and
  deflection; the scar stories get better; people trust him less without saying so.
- **`soul.restraint` (restraint ↔ abandon).** High: in charged scenes, Jack sees the line clearly and the narration
  dwells on the beauty of not-crossing; the "step back" option is present and dignified. Low: his body leads; options
  to escalate appear; the step-back option becomes harder to see (sometimes absent under low resolve too, but never all
  three — the player always has an exit).

## 29. Traits (firm up through choices)
- **Wit** — the quick line; unlocks riposte options in duels and banter; too much and people stop believing him.
- **Sincerity** — saying what he means; unlocks unguarded options; Lena's trust rises fastest here.
- **Charm** — the social lubricant; unlocks talking his way in/out; Theo's favorite.
- **Candor** — telling the hard truth to others; unlocks blunt options; friction rises.
- **Recklessness** — the dare; unlocks risky options (roofs, cliffs, drives to LA, punches); raises heat; lowers resolve.
- **Control** — the steady hand; unlocks restraint and de-escalation options; Fr. Osei notices.
Gate an option at `trait.X>=1` in early S1, `>=2` by midseason, `>=3` late.

## 30. Relationship axes on the page
- **Trust** — how much they tell Jack. Low: they deflect, lie lightly, leave out the middle of stories. High: they say the
  thing first; they call him at 3 a.m.; they let him see them ugly.
- **Warmth** — how much they like him. Low: politeness, last names, no teasing. High: nicknames, teasing, food, touch on
  the arm, being saved a seat.
- **Heat** — romantic/sexual charge (Lena primarily; Sol, Paige, Becca, Laney flirt-only). Low: banter without charge.
  High: silences that mean something; physical awareness in the prose; she stands closer; the duels get personal.
- **Friction** — the productive or destructive grit. Low: easy, maybe boring. Mid: sparring, which for Jack and Lena is
  love. High: real anger; they avoid him or seek him out to fight; Lena's sentences get formal.
- **Combinations:** Lena high heat + high friction + low trust = dangerous and doomed (for now); high trust + high warmth
  + mid friction + slow-rising heat = the endgame groove.

## 31. Duels in story terms
- A duel is a **real argument with stakes**. Each scene: Lena's move (in prose, a real argument), then 3–4 of Jack's moves:
  **Press** (rigor), **Riposte** (wit), **Concede** (heart), **Reframe**, **Personal** (wins, hurts).
- **Lena scores too** (`duel.<id>.lena`). She should land at least two clean hits per duel regardless of player choices.
- **Outcomes** branch on scores: rigor-led win (she respects him; friction up), wit-led win (warmth and heat up), heart-led
  (trust up; she tells him something true), Personal (a win that costs trust; she remembers).
- Duel topics in S1: Lip service (E01), whether Hale's soul argument is circular (E02), labor and the bailout (E03), moral
  luck (E05), the Anglican ordinariates (E11), Flannery O'Connor's violence (E12), postcards and pride (E14), the Manhattan
  Declaration (E16), Advent vs. Christmas creep (E19), Hale's oral exam (E22).

## 32. Journal / clue board rules
- Journal text is Jack's voice and Jack's knowledge. Mix true clues with texture so the board never shouts. In the pilot,
  clue-type entries are few and innocuous: "The rule about the Point," "A plaque at the shrine," "The guy at the end of the
  driveway" (as a note), "Theo, 5 a.m." (as a note).
- Person cards update as Jack learns. Lena's card begins "The girl on the porch."
