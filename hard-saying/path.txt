== s01
6am fire lane! Dress warm. Don’t eat. It’s a surprise (it’s a small one)
That came in last night at 9:02, and it is the whole reason Jack is standing in the Delgado fire lane at two minutes to six on a Friday in full dark, in the Carhartt over the Mooney hoodie, with nothing in his stomach. The red Ranger is already there, idling, because she is always early and it’s a ranch thing. Idaho plates. The Rathdrum feed-store sticker. St. Isidore on the visor with his plow and his angel, keeping an eye on the road.
Two coffees ride on the bench seat in a cardboard tray from the Portolá dining hall. “It opens at six,” Becca says, handing him one as he climbs in. “It opened at a quarter to. I asked them to.” Jack looks at her for some sign of whether that’s a joke and gets nothing; she’s checking her mirror. The cab smells like coffee and hay and the inside of a work glove. There’s a Rathdrum Feed & Seed receipt curling on the dash, a Glory Be card rubber-banded to the visor under St. Isidore, and her Ropers working the clutch like she learned on a hay truck.
Over the Lagoon, high in the west, the moon is still up, lopsided, going pale over the Delgado roofs.
She takes Ocean Road toward Campus Point with the heater roaring and the radio on low. KCBX, 90.1, out of San Luis Obispo: *Morning Edition*, and a reporter in Oslo, where it is already the middle of the afternoon, saying the Norwegian Nobel Committee has decided to award the Peace Prize for 2009 to President Barack Obama. The radio reads the citation: *for his extraordinary efforts to strengthen international diplomacy and cooperation between peoples.* The coffee is too hot to drink. Jack holds it on his knee and hasn’t had a sip.
“Oh!” Becca says. “Good for him!” Then she glances over to see what he thinks.
>> “For what? He’s been president nine months.”
Becca thinks about it honestly for a quarter of a mile, both hands on the wheel, the turn signal ticking for Campus Point. Then she has it. “Maybe it’s in advance,” she says. “Like a ribbon at the fair before the calf’s done anything, so it’ll walk right.”
At the light by the Marine Lab turnoff Jack takes his first sip.
== s01b
The Marine Lab sits low at the root of Campus Point with its back to the bluff, and out here before dawn the only sounds are the surf below and the pump behind a steel door marked AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL, a hum Jack can feel in his fillings. Becca lets them in with a key on a 4-H lanyard and writes 6:19 on a paper time sheet tacked by the light switch, in block numerals, in the IN column.
Inside is one low cinderblock room that smells like salt and the bottom of a bait bucket, and a concrete tank built like a tide pool at knee height. Giant green anemones open like something spilled. Ochre stars, purple and orange, clamped to the rock. Turban snails. At the far end a bat ray drifts and turns, slow as a dropped scarf, and everywhere along the bottom the hermit crabs go about their business in their borrowed houses.
“Thursday afternoons and Friday six to eight,” Becca says. “Work-study. That’s how I could sign Thomas out.” She puts a drop of tank water on the glass of a little refractometer, holds it up to the fluorescent tube, squints. “Thirty-three.” Then the thermometer: fifty-eight degrees. Both go on the clipboard. Then a Tupperware labeled SQUID — FRI in marker.
Jack watches her be good at something. The marker on her knuckles from lettering somebody’s poster. The braid down her back, still damp. The freckles she doesn’t cover, the sunburn along the part in her hair. She goes down the tank on a folded towel, a piece of squid to every star.
She finds Thomas by the chip in his borrowed whelk shell and lifts him out on her palm, where he pulls everything in at once. She holds him up to the fluorescent tube. “Everybody, this is Thomas. He’s been to church.” She sets him back down by the rock and he unfolds and walks off.
“You said at the Blessing your sister drew you a crab, and her birthday’s Wednesday,” she says. “So I sent her Thomas.” Ballpoint, on a card, every leg; and on the back six true facts about hermit crabs, of which she recites two. “They aren’t true crabs. And when a big shell washes up they line up by size, biggest to littlest, and everybody moves up one. Like my brothers with jeans.”
Jack asks how she had the address. “You gave me your address for my Christmas cards. I do them in October.” She mailed it Wednesday, on Rosie’s birthday, though it would get there late. “Birthdays count for a week at our house.” Jack had no idea. His own card went last Thursday: a whale, and his ballpoint crab, and THIS IS A CRAB under it. “Now she has two,” he says. “Mine’s the bad one.” “Yours has a *caption*.”
She drops a piece of squid in front of the biggest star and watches it decide. “Nobody ever thanks you for praying for them,” she says. “They thank you for the casserole. I showed my mom.”
By twenty to seven the high window has gone gray. She tips the clipboard so he can read the page under the tank log.

“Lena told Mrs. Fitch at the 12:10,” Becca says, “and Mrs. Fitch told Coffee and Donuts, and Coffee and Donuts told me.” She can’t wait to meet him. “I’ve been praying for them since September. Lena and Wes.” She looks up from the squid. “Is that weird?” She holds out the Tupperware before he can answer, and the anemone nearest him opens a little wider.

She narrates it to him as she goes, salinity and temperature and who ate, like a letter home: number four ate, number six is thinking about it, the bat ray is sulking and she’ll let him. When she catches him watching she isn’t shy about it for a second. “You can watch,” she says. “I’m good at this.”
At 7:58 she writes the time in the OUT column, and the Ranger takes him back to the Delgado fire lane. She leans across the seat as he gets out: “Tonight! Laney’s bonfire! I’m bringing s’mores.”

By lunch the Fishbowl TV has been saying the same thing for ten hours. CNN, on a loop: the committee in Oslo, the stunned man at the podium, and the President in the Rose Garden at the top of the morning, calling it “a call to action,” and back to Oslo. On the long couch Doug Pruitt is asleep under a thermodynamics textbook open across his face like a pup tent.
Theo is on the arm of the other couch eating a pomegranate with a spoon, and has been delighted since breakfast. “Didn’t see it coming,” he says. “Neither did he. That’s not a prize, Lindqvist. That’s a cliffhanger.”
Margot is at the far end with the *Tide* laptop on her knees and the red Dymo badge on her lapel, TIDE, and her Docs on the coffee table. She observes Leif Erikson Day. It has been a presidential proclamation every October ninth since Lyndon Johnson, she tells Jack, and she keeps it instead of the other one. Jack has already seen. “It’s a prize for not being the last guy,” Margot says of the Nobel, typing. “The most reasonable thing a Norwegian’s done since Ibsen.”

Since LIAR. xo at 4:43 on a Thursday morning last week, Laney has become the person he texts. That is the only excuse Jack will ever have for what he does next. He tells her Becca’s coming to everything this weekend. He wants her to have a good time. He wants to *do* something, not just stand there. Does Laney have an idea.
Laney lights up like a runway. “Sugar,” she says. “A girl like that has never had a *poem* in her life.” Out loud. Tonight, at the bonfire. In front of God and everybody. About her eyes, and it has to rhyme. “And when you get to the end — this is the important part — you go down on one knee.”
“Laney.”

“Uh-huh,” Laney says.
Her glance goes over his shoulder to the arm of the couch, quick. On the arm of the couch Theo has put down the pomegranate, and his thumbs are going on the iPhone. >> “Laney, that’s insane.”

== s03
The plan was Kevin’s Odyssey: Kevin driving, Lena up front, Jack in the back to lift whatever Wes brought, fifty-five minutes to Santa Barbara for a 5:25 out of Phoenix, leaving at a quarter to four to be early, because Lena. The plan is sitting on its front passenger rim. The tire has gone down flat and puddled out onto the asphalt, and on the hood, set upright at the base of the windshield, is the valve cap, like a tip.
On the back glass, in Lena’s white La Vang polish, it still says 4–0.
Kevin walks once around the van and comes back to the tire. “Who lets the air out of a *minivan*?”
Theo arrives across the lot jingling the SL keys, carrying a sheet of foam board under one arm. On it, hand-lettered in black calligraphy with swashes on the capitals: ARCENEAUX. He takes in the tire with real sorrow. “Tragic,” he says. “Take mine —” He looks two rows over at the champagne-gold 450SL. “Oh. Two seats.” He pauses, and enjoys the pause. “I’ll go. I’ll bring him to you with a bow on.”
“Theodore,” Lena says.
The SL is out of the lot and gone up Ocean Road before she gets to the second syllable.
So: the spare, from under the van floor, a donut that has never been out in daylight. The scissor jack. Jack learned this on the Silverado in the driveway on Glenwood Avenue with his father standing over him with a coffee: lefty-loosey, and don’t strip it, because we’re not buying another one. He breaks the lug nuts loose with his foot on the wrench. Kevin takes them one at a time and zips them into the pocket of his quarter-zip and keeps a hand over the pocket, an usher with the collection. When the tire comes off, Kevin runs his palm around the tread, cheerful as a resident on rounds. “Patient’s stable. No nail. No cut. No history of trauma.” He goes around the tread again, and the cheer goes out of him. “There’s nothing *wrong* with it.”
Lena is on the curb in a blue sundress and the Justins, with the oatmeal cardigan, which she keeps taking off and putting back on; when she sets it down beside her something small and metal in the pocket clicks against the concrete. Jack has seen her take Hale apart across a seminar table and ring the bell at Sully’s till the owner surrendered. He has never seen her nervous. She gets up twice to check her face in the van’s side window and sits back down.
“He’s never been west of Houston,” she says.
Jack asks her about the Nobel. She has a take; she always has a take. “Infant baptism,” Lena says. “You give the grace first and pray the child grows into it.” She tucks her hair behind her ear and untucks it. “The Norwegians are Lutherans. They’d hate the comparison.”
Jack is on his knees at the wheel, getting the donut onto the studs, when she says, to the top of his head, “Becca’s status says she’s the luckiest girl in Portolá.” He keeps threading the first nut. A pause. “I’m glad, Jack.” When he looks up she’s looking right at him.
>> “Danny says hey.”
Kevin has gone around to the back of the van with the flat. Jack says it to the lug nut. “Danny says hey. He put it in his last letter.”
Lena stops with the cardigan halfway onto one arm. “Hey back,” she says. “Tell him the Saints are four and oh and I expect him to know that. Tell him I said it myself.” And there it is, her smile, and it isn’t for the parking lot.

By 4:25 the donut is on, the flat is in the back, and Theo, Kevin calculates, has forty minutes’ start and a V8. So they wait. Lena won’t go inside. “If I go inside I’ll change my dress again.”
Package acquired. He’s taken my Wayfarers. I like him.
At 6:15 she is still on the curb, the cardigan on, and the sun is going down orange behind the eucalyptus.

They hear it before they see it: Karen O, coming up Ocean Road out of a 1979 dashboard that was never built for her, through the cord of the cassette adapter Theo runs his iPod into. Then the SL turns in off the road with the top down and the sign wedged behind the seats, Theo driving with one wrist, and in the passenger seat, in Theo’s Wayfarers, a big sunburned man holding a Mason jar up over the windshield like a trophy. The jar is empty. The electrical tape on the lid has been cut and taped back down, and THE PACIFIC. (A PREVIEW.) is still on it in Lena’s capitals.
“I drank it,” Wes says.
Lena runs. She runs the length of three parking spaces in the Justins and he is out of the car before it has finished stopping, and they meet by the SL’s back fender and kiss hello in the lot, his hand flat between her shoulder blades, the jar held out of the way behind her.
Kevin picks the valve cap up off the Odyssey’s hood, turns it over once, and zips it into the pocket of his quarter-zip, where the lug nuts were.
Theo lifts a green duffel out of the trunk, carries it to the curb like a bellhop, sets it down, and stands beside it. “He called my car *ma’am* at a stoplight in Goleta,” Theo tells Kevin. “I may never recover.”
Lena turns her head inside Wes’s arm. “Two and a half hours, Theodore. On a *curb*. In my good dress.” She lets go of Wes long enough to point. “Oh, Magdalena is *going* to say something. Not today. When you least expect it. At *Mass*.” Theo bows from the waist. “I owe you a kidney.” “You owe me *both* of them.” Wes laughs so hard he has to put the jar down on the hood.
Wes is six-two and easy in all of it, with shoulders that look like they have pulled a boat through something, a sunburn that stops at the sleeve of his T-shirt, sandy hair cut short. When he grins there’s a chip off one front tooth. His Saints cap has a brim bent so long it’s nearly a tube; Lena touches it as she goes by. “He’s been bending that since oh-six.” He pushes Theo’s Wayfarers up onto the cap and looks around him at all of it: the eucalyptus, the dry gold hills going pink, the Lagoon. “October,” he says, “and nobody’s sweating.” Then he looks at Lena, the whole of her, from the boots up. “Look at you,” he says. “You look like Easter, dawlin’.” His vowels are New Orleans but not hers — slower, further back, in no hurry at all.
Lena keeps his hand in hers and brings him the last ten feet. “This is Jack.”
“You’re the Ohio guy.” Wes puts out his hand. “She said you were a jagoff. That means she likes you. I’ve known her since she had a retainer.” His grip has lifted things for a living.
>> “Welcome to the Pacific.”
Wes takes that seriously. He looks past Jack, past Delgado, toward the sound beyond the Lagoon. “Where’s it at?” he says. “I want to touch it.” “Tomorrow,” Lena says, pulling him by the hand. “It’s a *threat*, Wesley.”
Then they start up the walk toward Delgado, Theo trailing with the sign, Wes’s arm across Lena’s shoulders, his head turned toward the sound of the water.

THREE NIGHTS MAXIMUM.
THIS MEANS YOU.
—P. Raman, RA
Wes signed for Friday, Saturday and Sunday in a slow, round hand, and hung the pen back on its chain.
Shipwreck Cove, at ten past nine, belongs to Laney. Laney said there’d be a fire every Friday, and she keeps her word in lighter fluid: driftwood and two broken pallets in the ring of black rocks, burning high and loud, the sparks climbing the bluff toward the stairs and the dead Christmas lights along the rail, dark as a necklace in a drawer. Past the fire the schooner’s ribs stand up out of the sand, lit on the near side and gone on the far. No moon. More stars over the water than seems strictly reasonable, and the surf out there in the dark, loud and invisible. Down the beach Pi Lam has a fire of its own and is shrieking at it.
Everybody is on the logs. Kevin, in a navy quarter-zip, feeds the fire on a schedule only he knows. Margot has the one folding chair, because she brought it. Theo has the 5D in his lap with the lens cap on, and is watching Wes. Becca is beside Jack. And on the far side of the fire, against Wes, with his Tulane sweatshirt pulled down over her hands, Lena.
Becca has brought s’mores. She has brought them in gallon Ziplocs labeled in marker on strips of masking tape: GRAHAMS. HERSHEY’S. MARSHMALLOWS (NEW BAG). STICKS (WASHED). Laney lowers her Solo cup to read the tape. “She labeled the marshmallows.”
“Laney,” Lena says across the fire, and means it. “Be nice.”
“So nobody gets the stale ones!” says Becca, and hands Laney a stick. Washed. Laney takes it.
Laney’s iPod dock sits on the cooler, and Taylor Swift is working her way through “Love Story” toward the part with the ring. Margot raises both eyebrows at the cooler and holds them up through the key change.
And Wes is easy. Jack came down the stairs with a whole plan for disliking him, and it doesn’t make it to the sand. Wes talks Saints the way Nonna talks saints: who did what, and when, and what it cost. “Worst Sunday of the year. Four and oh and nobody to play.” He was in the Dome last Sunday for Sharper — “ninety-nine yards, and he was laughing by the fifty” — and in 1980, nine years before Wes was born, his daddy and Mr. Tony Broussard went to the Dome with paper bags over their heads. “One and fifteen. My daddy still has his bag. Mama won’t let it in the house.”
“We had our own bag,” Jack says. “It was the whole city.” The Browns are oh and four. Wes wants to know who’s playing quarterback. “That’s the question,” Jack says. “Anderson or Quinn. It’s been the question since August. The answer is no.”
Then, without either of them finding the turn, it’s levees and Lordstown. “The Corps built them wrong,” Wes says, poking the fire with Kevin’s stick. “I’m gonna build them right.” And Jack, who doesn’t say this to people, says it to him: twenty-six years on the line, Cavaliers and then Cobalts, laid off since June, a list at the union hall for the new small car. “Next year, maybe.” Wes’s daddy has welded hulls at Avondale for thirty years, and he says *Avondale* the way Jack says *Lordstown*. Becca holds out a marshmallow, and Jack takes it.
“He thanked me for the sign,” Theo tells Jack, low, the way you’d report a crime. “He *kept* the sign.”
Margot, from her chair, puts Wes through the Army Corps of Engineers: the 17th Street Canal, the London Avenue Canal, who designed the floodwalls, who signed off, and who, specifically, still has a job. Wes answers slowly, with names and years, and twice says, “I don’t know, and that’s a problem.” The second time, Lena comes up out of the sweatshirt.
“I know,” she says. “Everybody still has a job, Margot. Fifty-some breaches, eighty percent of the city under water, and the Corps’ own report calls it *a system in name only* — their words, page one, I will mail you the page. And you can’t sue them. Flood Control Act of 1928, section three: *no liability of any kind*. Of *any kind*. They wrote that in after the ’27 flood so nobody could ever send them a bill.” She is up on her knees on the log now, sleeves flapping, the fire all down one side of her. “So he doesn’t know who still has a job, because the answer is everybody, and he’s going to go fix their walls anyway, for less money, because somebody from Gentilly ought to.” Wes leans back on his hands and lets her go, grinning, a man who has heard it before and would pay to hear it again.
Margot sits back. To Jack, not lowering her voice much: “Structurally, he’s sound. I hate that.”

>> “Lena says you had a boat.”

== s04b
At ten to ten Wes digs a scuffed black flip phone out of his jeans, holds it close to his face to find what he wants, and props it open on top of Laney’s dock where the iPod was. A fiddle saws out of it, thin and sweet, and a dead man’s voice crying something in French. “Lena’s Mémère makes everybody learn it,” Wes tells Becca, and holds out his hand to Lena. “Come on, dawlin’.”
He waltzes her by the ribs. Jack knows the song. He knows the sand too: nine nights ago, a few yards from where Wes is turning her now, he learned this, long, short, short, with his hand at her back and the moon on the water. Tonight there’s no moon, only the fire on one side and the black timbers on the other. Wes bounces. Cajuns don’t bounce; Jack has this on authority. Over Wes’s shoulder Lena’s face comes round with the turn, and finds the ribs, and comes back.
Then Becca has him up by both hands. “Come on. I can’t sit through a waltz, it’s physically impossible.” She puts his right hand where it goes and takes his left, and counts him in under her breath, and on the first long step it turns out he doesn’t need counting. Long, short, short. Becca leans back to look at their feet. “Jack. Where’d you learn *that*?”
Over Lena’s head, across the firelight, Wes’s face is turned their way, and stays turned.
>> Don’t talk. Dance.
He doesn’t answer. He turns her instead, and Becca lets the question go — she’s counting now under her breath, *one, two, three*, grinning down at her own Ropers — and over her shoulder Wes’s eyes stay on Jack until the turn takes him the other way.
Then the song ends, and they’re all back on the logs.

At twenty-five to eleven Laney gets up, walks all the way around the fire, and puts the big yellow Maglite out of Kevin’s glove box into Jack’s hand. “Now, Shakespeare.”
Theo’s lens cap comes off. The 5D comes up. Laney has her pink BlackBerry out too, held sideways, the little red light on. Kevin shuts his eyes. “Respectfully,” he says, “this is a hostage situation.”

Lena sits against her window with her knees drawn up and watches the strawberry fields go by, row on row. Jack has his own window, and the same fields.
